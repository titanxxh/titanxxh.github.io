titanxxh's 2016 Board Game Review

titanxxh2017-01-012026-08-23

BG Stats

The BG Stats app records board-game sessions and provides statistics, making it the perfect assistant for an annual review.

Across 2016, I played 105 different games, a number that surprised even me. BG Stats currently does not recognize relationships between base games and expansions very well, however, counting non-standalone expansions as separate games. That is annoying, though I rarely play with expansions, so this figure should remain fairly accurate. My H-index was only 6, meaning just six games reached six plays, so the 2016 10 × 10 Challenge definitively failed again. There are simply too many new games these days. BGG’s database grows by almost 10,000+ titles every year, the number of halls at Essen increases annually, and chasing new releases is a consensus within my gaming circle—yes, President Wen, I mean you. Combined with the drastic loss of free time after beginning full-time work, these factors have made repeated plays increasingly rare. I miss my university years, when we could play Ora et Labora dozens of times.

Breaking the numbers down, I played 158 sessions during the first four months, before starting the job, though online Agricola accounted for a large share. After I joined the company, only 35 percent of the year’s plays remained; May and September both recorded zero sessions… This will probably deteriorate further in 2017, so instead of another 10 × 10 Challenge, I plan to attempt 100 × 1. After Club A stopped hosting open events, I never had a fixed venue. Chengshan Road now looks likely to become the place I visit most often, mainly because it is close to both E and me. As a standard Eurogamer, more than half my plays were at four players, demonstrating just how deeply the common overseas family structure of two parents and two children has influenced German-style games.

E, Bowl King, and the Editor-in-Chief became my best gaming buddies this year—last year it was Xiaoda, E, and Bowl King. I thank them for their company, especially the Editor-in-Chief, who read the rules alone and taught us T.I.M.E Stories and Pandemic Legacy. Sadly, after I stopped going to Club A, I never saw the Editor-in-Chief again T_T… Bowl King left to study in Hong Kong, so I barely saw him during the second half of the year. E was probably still the person I met most often. In a year without a regular venue, we even held one session inside Ctrip’s headquarters and visited the legendary senior executive’s office—the one whose routine is running at work and playing with his child after work. I also thank all our other friends and everyone on the editorial team. I even saw C this year, although she is really E’s fan…

The Fallen King — Agricola

Online Agricola on iOS was unquestionably my most-played game of 2016, accounting for a quarter of all sessions. I just checked the BGG rankings and found that Agricola has fallen out of the top ten, which is deeply saddening. On this day last year, Twilight Struggle, which had held BGG’s number-one spot for five years, was displaced by Pandemic Legacy. Another year has passed, and the second-generation ruler of the board-game world, Agricola, has now left the top ten too. Look at the games that overtook it: the American-favorite Star Wars setting of Star Wars: Rebellion, Kickstarter darling Scythe, lightweight two-player strategy game 7 Wonders Duel, and even its old companion Stefan Feld’s The Castles of Burgundy. Have these games truly surpassed Agricola in gameplay? I doubt it. The main reason is that the people submitting BGG ratings are no longer the “geeks” implied by Geek Rating. More and more mainstream players are joining BGG and giving newer games high scores, while older games suffer simply from being born first. Their rating-distribution histograms show this clearly. Yet the expansion of the board-game audience is also what produced the rapid growth mentioned at the beginning, so perhaps this is historically inevitable. No reign lasts forever.

Every online Agricola session occurred in the first four months. During that period, I met the experts in a Chinese iOS Agricola group and realized how low-level my previous play had been. It felt like ten years earlier, after I had played Heroes of Might and Magic for years and first opened the Gamer’s Home website. Games against experts repeatedly transformed my understanding, especially those against Koko. His fluid, effortless play crushed us even after we forced two Begging cards on him—actually, the three of us combined to deny him food for a round. Ordinary-looking cards became miraculous in Koko’s hands, and even cards we considered garbage turned from ugly ducklings into swans. I later learned that the best platform for online Agricola is PlayAgricola, whose many new cards almost transform the game itself. Strategies without Family Growth are everywhere. One has to admire both the players’ limitless imagination and the vitality of this old game. To this day, President Wen, Xiaoda, and I remain devoted to Agricola. Despite the flood of new releases, we are willing at any moment to pull out the complete game and all expansions for a face-to-face session. They even brought new Agricola cards back from Essen. This year marks the game’s tenth anniversary, and Uwe surely will not miss such a perfect opportunity to make money. A tenth-anniversary edition containing a thousand cards is reportedly ready. What are Agricola devotees waiting for?

Best Party Game — The Codenames Series


The black—adult—edition of Codenames

Codenames is the best party game for both laughter and strategy. Its number-one position among BGG party games is thoroughly deserved, and our 33 sessions prove it. We played Xiaoda’s English copy at Club A until it was worn out. Even after the initial novelty faded, whenever a gathering is about to end and half an hour remains, we pull out Codenames without hesitation and complete one tense yet brisk game.

This year brought Codenames: Pictures. It moves faster, removes language dependence, and is better for teaching new players and family gatherings. Yet after our limited number of plays, everyone agreed that the picture edition was less interesting than the word edition. That is fascinating: images appear to contain more information, yet offer less than words. Perhaps pictures are more concrete while words have wider associations. Books and films create a similar contrast.

A Worthy New Number One — Pandemic Legacy


A gathering in Montreal: the four leaders meet to exchange information, research the final cure, and prepare for victory.

Like many veteran players, the Editor-in-Chief, E, President Wen, and I doubted PL—Pandemic Legacy—as it shot up the rankings. After fighting through “ten months,” however, we had to admit that the new number one deserved its position. The difficulty grows gradually, and the unfolding plot generates real tension. During my first few months with the team, we swept through every monthly scenario in a single victory. By September and October, however, E and President Wen collectively slacked off and caused us to lose; I even sacrificed my own character to force a win. In our final month, a seemingly minor mistake by the Editor-in-Chief and me directly triggered outbreaks across the entire map, leaving little hope of a later victory. Although I did not complete the full twelve-month campaign—I do not know whether they finished it afterward—I strongly recommend finding four people and experiencing a true “pandemic.”

How an Expansion Skeptic Changed His Mind — Power Grid: The Stock Companies

People who know me may know that I dislike expansions. When a game receives one, my opinion of it generally declines. In recent years, the formerly simple and self-contained world of board games has caught the video-game industry’s DLC habit. Content that could have appeared together is deliberately withheld for a supplement or expansion, which is deeply irritating. But Power Grid: The Stock Companies made me reconsider the relationship between an expansion and its base game.

The Stock Companies expansion changes the classic structure of this old game—not in the inconsequential way of earlier map expansions, but by changing its objective. It retains the core play while shifting the focus toward stocks. It adds three variants, all involving stocks. They cannot compare with the many heavier railway-and-stock games, of course, but they play briskly and satisfyingly. Most importantly, they reduce the headache-inducing exact calculation, preserve interaction on the map, and introduce competition over companies. You can even win without operating a company yourself. (What I will not tell you is that the real reason this game addicted me is my perfect four-for-four record, hahaha.)

Best of the Year — Great Western Trail!

Great Western Trail looks like a railway game, but players are cowboys driving cattle, so we affectionately call it “Driving the Big Cattle.” If I could recommend only one new game from 2016, this would be it. The flow is simple, but the variations and scoring paths are numerous. That alone would not make it stand out amid the recent Eurogame trend toward multiple point-scoring routes—think of WYG’s games, Mombasa, and so on.

What made me immediately declare it the year’s best on my very first play? Simple: in Great Western Trail, the players completely control the game’s rhythm. Most board games use a fixed number of rounds. Some have no fixed round count and instead use an end-game trigger, but the number of player turns is effectively fixed. In Great Western Trail, players control the speed at which they enter Kansas City and thus determine their own number of turns, while the frequency with which everyone reaches Kansas City collectively determines the game’s pace. Its elements are also tightly integrated—the previous game that gave me this feeling was Nippon, though that has severe balance problems. Players feel great freedom of action, yet interaction remains strong. It provided my best play experience of the year, making it my 2016 game of the year.

Other Recommendations

  2. Railroad Revolution
  3. Mombasa
  4. Stronghold, Second Edition
  5. A Feast for Odin
  6. Alchemists + expansion
