[A Life in Board Games] Episode 7: The Art and Way of Agricola

titanxxh2017-05-012026-08-22

The Trough of Fatigue

Time is always the greatest enemy. No matter how deeply we once loved something, we have to admit that fatigue eventually arrives. What reaches an extreme must reverse; what flourishes must decline. In one sense, my love for something feels a little like this technology maturity curve.

As time passes, a board gamer’s enthusiasm for the hobby follows roughly the same curve. At first, everything looks fresh and the newcomer frantically chases new releases. Then passion gradually cools. Current designs begin to reveal familiar patterns, and every new game recalls some old classic. A trough follows, when fewer and fewer games seem genuinely fun. Yet only after passing through that process and washing away the glitter is a player willing to look back at older games: classics that were never explored deeply during the frantic pursuit of novelty—such as Agricola. So today, let us properly discuss this former titan of the board-game world.

Agricola is a standard Eurogame that demands both short-term decisions and long-term strategy, and it excels at both. As in every traditional abstract strategy game, the outcome is theoretically determined from the beginning; there are simply too many game states for even a computer to calculate every decision. That does not prevent generation after generation of masters from continuing to explore their craft—although AlphaGo recently dealt humanity a heavy blow. Agricola is not a traditional abstract game. It contains randomness and hidden information, but limits both so that replayability increases without allowing the system to escape control. One might say it preserves the soul of the Eurogame as fully as possible.

Three Levels of Understanding

Since the end of the year before last, I have been playing Agricola on iOS with Xiaoda, Boss Wen, experts from the Agricola group, and random opponents. By now, I have played nearly two hundred games, most of them extremely slow asynchronous sessions with a one-week limit. Combined with earlier face-to-face play at university, my understanding of the game has passed through three levels.

  1. Seeing water as water, and water as water
    This stage covers roughly the first twenty to thirty games. It is easy to fall into fixed patterns and believe you have grasped the rhythm, only to discover quickly that the strategy is flawed. You are not yet familiar with the cards, so your judgment of their strength usually rests on limited personal experience or guides written on some website. Even when you win at this stage, your score is rarely high—perhaps just over forty. If your routine is blocked, you may score only in the twenties.
    Incidentally, Agricola’s essence lies in the standard four-player game. Two- and three-player Agricola are different games, and many cards perform poorly with fewer players, producing a weaker experience. Overall, however, Agricola still balances player counts better than many modern designs.
  2. Seeing mountains not as mountains, and water not as water
    Depending on a person’s insight, this stage may last about fifty games. If you regularly face experts such as Teacher koko, perhaps ten games are enough to make a breakthrough. Based on my own limited experience, I would not dare claim that I have completely passed this second level.
    Here, players gradually recognize the limits of their own understanding, usually through defeat after defeat. Strategies that once seemed powerful lose their shine, while cards you considered useless appear formidable in somebody else’s hands. After dozens of repetitions, you begin trying new approaches and boldly use cards you had never considered. Most of the time you still lose, of course, but once or twice you win and discover that your final score has reached a new height: more than fifty.
  3. Seeing mountains once more as mountains, and water as water
    After repeatedly trying new ideas, failing, and being proven wrong, you gradually recognize the game’s central idea: action efficiency. What determines your efficiency across an entire game is neither some supposed measure of card strength nor a fixed formula. It is the simplest, plainest quality, one that almost represents the soul of Eurogames: depth of thought.
    The Art of War says: those who win in the temple calculations before battle make many calculations; those who lose make few. Many calculations lead to victory, few to defeat—how much more certain is defeat with none at all. A player who reaches this level finds that even with the iOS edition’s limited card decks, EIKG, scoring fifty in a standard four-player game becomes relatively easy, though not every time; sixty may occasionally be broken. Most importantly, you know why you won and why you lost. That feeling is exceedingly rare. How many people go through life truly understanding how they live? As another saying has it: I searched for her a thousand times among the crowd; suddenly I turned, and there she stood where the lantern light was dim. Wang Guowei’s third realm resembles this in some respects.

Action Efficiency: The Way of the Farm

Let us examine the idea. What is action efficiency? Readers probably already have that question. Looking at Agricola globally, there are only two ways to earn a higher score: increase the number of your actions or improve their efficiency.

Beginners feel the importance of the first immediately when encountering a worker-placement game like Agricola. The mechanism explicitly tells players to grow their family and thereby gain more actions. This is why so much of Agricola’s banned-card list concerns family growth or room construction. Ignoring banned cards, growing your family one round earlier means gaining one additional action. In a standard four-player game, the last person to grow usually does so three rounds after the first. Statistics show that the first player to grow has a win rate as high as 40%, while each later player wins roughly 20% of the time. The first-family-growth player therefore seems to possess an enormous advantage, twice the win rate. Should everyone chase that position in every game? Clearly not. Even the leading player’s win rate remains below 50%, while all later positions are similar. There is also an intuitive demonstration: download the game and play against three Master-level AIs. They seem programmed always to build a room and grow first, going so far as to take a single reed. I trust that everyone can still defeat them easily.

In this extreme example, I defeated the computer with only two family members. Action efficiency is often the true key to victory.

Against human opponents, differences in action efficiency are not so enormous and usually appear in small details. The iOS game has an excellent log. At the end of a session, count how many inefficient actions you performed. An average game of Agricola provides about forty-two actions. A small shortfall in each accumulates across more than forty turns into a large divergence. How, then, do we increase efficiency? In one sentence: think more.

Returning to the extreme example above, I took only twenty-eight actions in the entire game, but synergies among occupations combined with the resources accumulated while the computers fought over family growth in the middle game made those actions effective.

Miscellaneous Notes

Note: this section exists only to keep the article from feeling too abstract. It will probably offer little value to the experts in our group. This resembles programming: simply reading without practicing accomplishes little. The Way described in the Tao Te Ching cannot be found inside the Tao Te Ching.

What Is Inefficient?

My list of inefficient actions includes:

  1. Using only half of any “and/or” or “and then/or” action—for example, taking Starting Player alone, sowing without baking bread, or renovating without playing an improvement
  2. Taking Day Laborer without a bonus
  3. Taking 4 or fewer clay
  4. Taking 2 or fewer reed, or reed you cannot use late in the game
  5. Taking RSF in the middle or late game
  6. Taking 3 or fewer wood
  7. Sowing fewer than two fields
  8. Taking grain or vegetables without a bonus
  9. Playing one improvement card
  10. Building only one room
  11. Taking 2 or fewer sheep, or 2 or fewer wild boars
  12. Fishing or Traveling Players for 5 or less food without a supporting ability
    Actions near the top are generally less efficient, but none of these rules is absolute. Occupations and improvements often transform an inefficient action into an efficient one. The one unshakably foolish move may be taking Starting Player with no other benefit.

How to Think

As noted earlier, the soul of a Eurogame lies in depth of thought. In Agricola, that can be divided into several key points:

  1. During the draft, fully consider turn order and the arrangement of your eight actions in the first period. Ensure that your cards cover the early, middle, and late game, ideally with strong individual cards at each stage rather than blindly chasing combinations.
  2. Before every action, consider whether your plan must change. When the plan requires many actions to complete, do not be tempted by an unrelated opportunity, such as taking six wood. Agricola is not a game in which you can decide one move at a time. Even in the first period, much must be planned.
  3. Build a food engine. The most direct way to improve action efficiency is usually to establish an effective food engine. Many kinds are effective and differ only slightly. You must still understand which are somewhat more efficient and which fit your plan.
  4. Act outside peak demand. When everyone fights over the first family growth, do something else, such as plowing or fencing. A good food engine can release its potential in the middle and late game.
  5. Calculate scores precisely after turn 12. Once the turn-12 action card is revealed, the game’s final random element disappears. Ignoring each player’s first picks during the card draft—usually strong occupations and minor improvements are played early rather than held this long—the final three rounds can theoretically be calculated completely.

Common Agricola Misconceptions

  1. You must secure the first family growth. Already discussed.
  2. Banned cards are overwhelmingly strong. Their power is not as great as imagined. Cards are often banned merely because they are annoying, such as Taster, or broadly useful, such as those related to family growth. Without improved action efficiency, one banned card offers little advantage. I have even seen a game where someone played Reed Hut in turn 3 and still finished last.
  3. A begging card means the game is over. Begging cards are certainly frustrating and are one reason many newcomers find Agricola unfriendly. But one or two are discouraging, not hopeless. I once believed otherwise until a game with Teacher koko. In turn 7, the other three of us blocked him and forced him to take two begging cards. He still finished second and lost by only a few points. Even now, I occasionally take one or two begging cards and can continue competing if I play well afterward. In a Eurogame, first place is not the only winner. The same is true in life.

    I lost this game but still decisively defeated banned-card king icanfarm.
  4. Plowing and fencing early lose tempo. Plowing has the same effect early or late. In most games, the first period contains enough spare actions for one plow, and even in the worst case it is no less useful than the inefficient actions above. Fencing requires an animal-breeding food engine and strong minor improvements to amplify its return, but can reverse the disadvantage of expanding your home late.
  5. Passive abilities trigger unreliably. Completely wrong! In most cases, cards saying “when another player does X, you may do Y” are good because they provide extra actions.
  6. Bonus-point cards are weak. Completely wrong! Agricola has a ceiling on ordinary scoring, and bonus cards raise that ceiling, though some also raise other players’ ceilings. In general, a bonus card that provides four wood and is completed produces three points. An average Agricola action comes nowhere near that return, even before counting the extra wood. All you need is one food to play the occupation and advance planning.
  7. Avoid improvements that require played occupations or improvements. Completely wrong! Such cards usually offer very high returns. The designer uses their restrictions merely to delay when they enter play, but designers never defeat the players. With proper planning, a fully utilized stable, mine, or similar occupation can generate value equal to several actions.

Afterword

I originally intended to write only some Agricola tips—the miscellaneous section. As I wrote, however, they kept connecting with my recent work and life, so I decided to add the article to the A Life in Board Games series. In truth, I merely wanted to show off without making it obvious.
