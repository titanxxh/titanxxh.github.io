A Journey Through Tanzania in 37 Photographs

titanxxh2016-02-172026-08-23

From Kilimanjaro to Lake Manyara, and from the Serengeti to Ngorongoro Crater, I did not want to revisit this journey as a simple chronological diary. Instead, titanxxh selected 37 photographs from the more than 2,000 I took. Through them, this article returns to the moments that moved me most.

“Baba Yetu” is written in Swahili, the local language. It is the theme song of Civilization IV and the first piece of video-game music to win a Grammy. To my surprise, our driver had heard it too.

  1. The Longest Night

    On the flight from Shanghai to Addis Ababa, we traveled in the same direction as the sun at roughly the same speed. We had boarded at midnight, yet experienced another twelve hours of darkness in the air. It felt as though this sleep should never end. Shortly before landing, I finally used my phone to record day and night changing places at an altitude of 40,000 feet.

  2. Uhuru Summit

    Mount Kilimanjaro—Kilimanjaro in Swahili, meaning “shining mountain”—lies in the Kilimanjaro Region of northeastern Tanzania near the Kenyan border. It is Africa’s highest mountain and is often called the Roof of Africa or King of Africa. Its highest point is Kibo, also known as Uhuru Peak, at 5,895 meters or 19,341 feet. Part of the mountain is protected as Kilimanjaro National Park and is listed as a World Heritage Site.[^1]
    My heart sank when we arrived at Kilimanjaro Airport. The day was overcast, so there was a good chance we would never see the mountain. Clouds concealed the entire massif as we drove toward Kilimanjaro National Park. Because our itinerary did not include entering the park, we soon turned back. Yet shortly before sunset, the tip of Uhuru Peak emerged. It was not very clear—perhaps my photography was simply poor—but I could not contain my delight and took this picture.

  3. Students in Tanzania

    We drove past a school just as classes ended and taught the children to say “Happy New Year.” They were very excited to see foreigners. Our driver later explained that Tanzania provided seven years of compulsory education, with hopes of extending it to eleven under the new president.

  4. The Road Uphill

    Most roads here are unpaved; only major routes receive a surface like this. I hope life here can gradually follow an uphill road too.

  5. Quadcopter

    A quadcopter, also called a quadrotor, is a multirotor aircraft with four rotors for hovering, maintaining attitude, and level flight. Unlike a fixed-wing aircraft, it rises through thrust from its rotors. All four are the same size and arranged almost symmetrically. In a simple design, changing the relative speeds of the rotors adjusts thrust at different points and counters each rotor’s torque, allowing the aircraft to hold an attitude or perform different maneuvers. This differs from a helicopter. A conventional helicopter has two rotors, and its tail rotor merely offsets torque from the main rotor and controls yaw.[^2]
    The film crew was highly professional and brought a quadcopter for aerial footage, though some countries apparently prohibit them. These were the first animals we encountered: zebras and giraffes beside the road. The crew launched the aircraft for the first time.

  6. Exhausted

    You understand…

  7. Half Dead, Half Alive

    I took this casually and happened to capture an acacia that was half withered and half flourishing. The contrast fascinated me. I wonder what misfortune befell its right side.

  8. Farm House Valley Lodge

    Our hotel on the second day was much better than the first. Every room had two beds, a child’s bed, and mosquito nets, while the grounds resembled a farm. Tanzania and Kenya take completely different approaches to conservation and tourism development. Tanzania strictly limits the number of incoming tourists, and the entire Serengeti contains only five hotels, so each hotel has excellent surroundings. This picture shows the swimming pool at the entrance, and pools are virtually standard equipment in local hotels. The natural consequence is higher prices.

  9. Grazier

    I captured this scene with my phone mainly because I was surprised to see a donkey here. Later we discovered camels too. Apparently a scientist introduced them and entrusted them to local people to see whether they could survive.

  10. Nature versus Science

    We came across an antelope carcass beside the road. Professor Wang stepped out of the vehicle to take a sample. Our driver said the distant stork would also eat carrion, so I photographed the moment.

  11. Helping Hand

    Throughout the trip, we met many European and American visitors. Most were elderly couples traveling by themselves, although someone this old was genuinely rare. At the rest area entering the Serengeti stood a small rocky hill. Everyone climbed it to look for lizards, myself included, and I happened upon this scene on the way up.

  12. Wandering

    This large stork strutted around the rest area without paying anyone the slightest attention. Animals here are not afraid of people. Protection is effective, no visitor or other person attacks them, and so they show no wariness toward humans.

  13. The Great Migration

    We had seen antelopes, wildebeest, buffalo, and zebras all along the route into Serengeti National Park, but this was the first time we found vast herds grazing together. Zebras eat the tops of the grass, wildebeest consume the lower parts, and gazelles eat the tender shoots that grow afterward. The whole ecology is wonderfully harmonious.

  14. Sunrise in the Serengeti

    On the third day, we stayed at Serengeti Lobo Wildlife Lodge. The hotel is distinctive, perched across several giant rocks, while its wooden corridors and staircases have a strikingly Chinese character. Best of all, two paths lead to separate high points from which visitors can photograph sunrise and sunset. I rose especially early the next morning and waited for dawn. At first, I thought the sun would remain hidden behind the clouds and be impossible to photograph, but nobody was willing to leave. At last it appeared, allowing me to capture this unforgettable image: sunrise over the Serengeti.

  15. May Peace Prevail on Earth

    A pillar stands outside Serengeti Lobo Wildlife Lodge, with this wish written on its four sides in four languages.

  16. Give Me Some Space

    Serengeti Lobo Wildlife Lodge is home to many rock hyraxes. They are round and adorable. Shortly before leaving, we found three pressed tightly together. The expression in this picture seems to say, “Move over—you are squashing me.”

  17. The Sleepy Family

    Lions hunt infrequently, so anyone who is not a documentary crew waiting in one place is unlikely to photograph them in motion. We were no exception. Even so, a whole family sleeping this sweetly on a rock was an engaging sight.

  18. Why Are You Here?

    What is that—a turtle? Yes, it is a tortoise. The Serengeti’s biodiversity is astonishing, and this unexpected creature is only the tip of the iceberg.

  19. Yawn

    Almost every small river here contains a community of hippos. Like lions, hippos are lazy and spend nearly the entire day soaking in water. When one finally opened its mouth wide, I captured the instant at once. We were told that a tourist from Shanghai once left his vehicle, whereupon an enraged mother hippo defending her calf rushed over and bit him in half.

  20. Arrogance

    Lizards usually walk with their heads down. Their bodies are so flat that photographs do not show them well. This phone image is not very sharp, but the colorful lizard raised its head proudly and made the scene far more vivid.

  21. A Weaving Weaver

    A male weaver bird’s nest is one way of attracting a mate. When a female arrives, she tries to damage the nest and test its stability. If she cannot break it, the nest is sturdy and dependable, so she stays; otherwise she abandons that male. While we waited at the rest area, this tree held many weaver nests, and this yellow bird happened to be building one.

  22. Loneliness

    A dead tree, solitary bird, and dark clouds compose a perfect scene of loneliness.

  23. Leopard

    We were extraordinarily fortunate to see all of Africa’s Big Five. We had already encountered many elephants, buffalo, and lions, but continued searching for a leopard and rhinoceros. Just before leaving the Serengeti, someone finally located a leopard resting in a tree. We could not approach, so I photographed from far away the only leopard we saw as a keepsake.

  24. All for One

    When one vehicle discovers something interesting, the driver usually spreads the news through various means. Some use radio; others exchange information verbally as vehicles pass. One cannot help admiring the drivers’ command of the grassland’s geography: in the Serengeti, with no road signs, they navigate entirely from memory. After this leopard was found, more than twenty vehicles arrived from every direction merely to catch a glimpse.

  25. Umbrella

    As acacias grew taller, giraffes’ necks grew longer too. Solitary acacias decorate the vast grassland. This specimen had an exceptionally classic shape, and the sky that day was perfectly clear, producing this photograph.

  26. Hourglass

    While we were driving, I suddenly noticed that a distant hill and the clouds beyond the window mirrored one another vertically. I quickly captured this “hourglass.”

  27. Outline

    From a hot-air balloon, we could look down over the grassland. Trees “protect” a small river along both banks, while only short grass grows beyond the water’s reach.

  28. Flames

    Our hot-air-balloon pilot was of mixed Canadian and Tanzanian heritage. He explained that a balloon cannot control its direction directly. Pilots instead learn the wind direction at different altitudes and adjust height to make small corrections. Intermittent bursts of flame kept us floating at a stable level.

  29. A Living Encyclopedia

    This was our driver. The job demands a great deal: besides driving for more than ten hours a day, drivers must identify the local animals. Most importantly, they possess eyesight far beyond that of ordinary people—vision like an eagle’s. While driving, they notice every distant irregularity: which bird is perched on a branch, or which animal hides in the grass. I cannot imagine developing those abilities without ten or twenty years of experience.

  30. What Are You Looking At?

    A weaver bird tilted its head and stared at me. I wanted to reply, “You. What about it?”

  31. Defense Mechanism

    When zebras sense danger, they gather together so each can watch another’s back. It is a defensive mechanism. Does it not feel like two people venturing deep into danger, then fighting back to back and entrusting their blind side to a companion?

  32. The Maasai

    We visited a Maasai village. The women sang and danced, while the men welcomed us by jumping vertically from a standstill. Maasai homes consist of only a few branches. They are so low and small that one must crouch to enter. Inside is room for only a bed and a fire, with no natural light. It is difficult to imagine how they survive. PS: their body odor was extremely strong…

  33. Simba

    It was rare enough to find a lion that was awake—and a male lion at that. I was thrilled.

  34. Scared Witless

    A zebra beside the road urinated in fright when it saw us approach.

  35. Geometric Aesthetics

    After touring Ngorongoro Crater, the slopes on both sides of the uphill road formed a perfect V, creating this photograph full of geometric beauty.

  36. The Starry Night of Ngorongoro Crater

    Every previous day, I had forgotten to photograph the African night sky. On our final night at Ngorongoro Crater, I took out the camera. Without a tripod, I used my phone as a support and recorded this perfect last night.

  37. Panoramic View of Ngorongoro Crater

    Save the best for last. If a trip to Africa leaves you only one day in Tanzania, do not hesitate: go directly to the Ngorongoro Conservation Area. Wildlife here is extraordinarily dense, and its diversity loses nothing to the Serengeti. We saw rare flamingos and the last member of the Big Five we had not yet encountered, the white rhinoceros. Ngorongoro Wildlife Lodge is the area’s oldest hotel. The next morning, I used my phone on the hotel balcony to capture this panorama of Ngorongoro Crater.
