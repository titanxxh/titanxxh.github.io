Touring a City Alone

titanxxh2016-04-182026-08-22

With full-time work about to begin, I slipped away to Beijing alone last week. I had wanted to explore the capital properly ever since attending a conference there the previous year. My only earlier visit had been when I was too young to remember anything, while last year’s schedule was so limited that I visited only the Forbidden City and the National Museum of China. As my final chance to relax before starting work, traveling alone felt free and pleasant.


Shamelessly borrowing a photograph of the Corner Tower to open the post—I really want an ultra-wide-angle lens.


A group portrait of my ticket stubs

Day 1: Peking University and Tsinghua University





The buildings on the Peking University campus are relatively old and in places even dilapidated, so I prefer Tsinghua’s environment and architectural design.

This was my first time booking accommodation through Airbnb. The host was a charming young woman. There was only one room, but it was clean and tidy and perfectly sufficient for sleeping.

Day 2: Temple of Heaven, Eight Great Hutongs, Prince Gong’s Mansion, Nanluoguxiang, and the National Library


The legendary douzhi, fermented mung-bean drink. It tasted like swill, but I forced myself to finish it. Beijing was the imperial capital and therefore contains every kind of regional cuisine. Its truly distinctive food is instead ordinary people’s street fare. Poor households made dishes that were either watery soups or used assorted scraps, such as quick-fried tripe, so the flavors can be strange.



A foreign head of state visited the Temple of Heaven while I was there, and the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests was placed under security restrictions.

This is said to have been one of Mei Lanfang’s stage costumes. Its color looks wrong because once an opera performer became famous, costumes were generally never washed. This is what resulted.

This setting has also appeared in many films, including Farewell My Concubine.

At noon I tried zhajiangmian and still did not find it tasty.

This was where Heshen stored his treasures in Prince Gong’s Mansion. Every room has a different lattice pattern on its window, and nobody but Heshen knew which pattern corresponded to which treasure inside.


Nanluoguxiang is much like Kuanzhai Alley in Chengdu. There is not much for someone alone—especially a man—to browse.

I was meeting friends for dinner that evening, so I did not go inside the library.

Day 3: Old Summer Palace, Summer Palace, and National Centre for the Performing Arts




The Old Summer Palace is a place of sorrow. Some of the twelve zodiac fountain heads remain overseas, while the whereabouts of others are unknown.


Victor Hugo’s furious condemnation of the burning of the Old Summer Palace!


The broken walls of the Great Fountain seem to possess a different kind of beauty.

The imperial seal is missing from the dragon’s claw at the Summer Palace, symbolizing the Guangxu Emperor’s lack of control over power.

Boating on Kunming Lake, with the Seventeen-Arch Bridge in view

The Summer Palace contains the world’s longest covered corridor. Its paintings are said to have been added voluntarily by students from the Central Academy of Fine Arts.

A panoramic view over Kunming Lake


The National Centre for the Performing Arts!

The boat from the Italian opera Aida

A prop from Aida—so magnificent.

At half price, a symphony concert cost only 25 yuan. I mainly went because my feet were dead after a day of walking. People in the capital are fortunate.

Day 4: Badaling Great Wall, Jingshan Park, Corner Tower, and National Centre for the Performing Arts




If you visit Beijing, do not go to the Great Wall. The value for money is far too low.

This is said to be the crooked tree from which the Chongzhen Emperor hanged himself.


A panoramic view of the Forbidden City from the summit of Jingshan Park. Once again, I was defeated by the lack of a wide-angle lens.

Oh, my beloved Corner Tower—why could I not create the same effect even after choosing sunset? Those photographers must have added a mountain of special effects. Duang!

I entered the National Centre for the Performing Arts again, once more after walking my legs off. Having a snack there and listening to other people perform was pleasant.

The play Rules of Survival felt a little like Twelve Angry Men. The entire story took place inside this small room.

Day 5: Capital Museum


One of the Capital Museum’s two major spring exhibitions featured the tomb of the Marquis of Haihun. Liu He was emperor for only twenty-seven days and later held the ranks of king and marquis—quite a legendary life.


The B1 level also contained an exhibition on the historical development of Beijing.


The other major spring exhibition marked the fortieth anniversary of the discovery of Fu Hao’s tomb. This is Fu Hao’s hairpin—or is it a knife?

A painting of the Two Immortals of Harmony and Union, telling the story of Hanshan and Shide. According to legend, they were as close as brothers and loved the same woman. When Hanshan learned on the eve of the wedding that Shide loved her too, he left home to become a monk. Shide also gave her up and went looking for Hanshan. After reuniting, both became monks and founded Hanshan Temple. From then on, the folk deity of harmony divided from one figure into two. They still look like monks: disheveled, smiling gods, one holding a lotus and the other a round box. In Chinese, “lotus” sounds like harmony and “box” like union, expressing a harmonious relationship.

At the porcelain exhibition, a volunteer explained the story behind this disc-shaped piece depicting the imperial kilns at Jingdezhen.

Afterword

I walked a complete circuit along the Tongzi River around the Forbidden City. I heard many Beijing residents explaining the city’s history to visitors and listened to conversations between older local men. Much of what they said was unofficial history, but their deep affection for the city was unmistakable. Beijing life is not the kind I am accustomed to, yet I would still gladly visit again if the opportunity arose—at the very least, there are exhibitions to see. Perhaps I will have no chance in the next few years, though. Any visit would be a business trip, without much free time.
