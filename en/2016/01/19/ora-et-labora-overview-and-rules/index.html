Ora et Labora Overview and Rules

titanxxh2016-01-192026-08-23

Ora et Labora, titanxxh’s single favorite game, now has a Chinese edition! So I am going to give a proper introduction to this culmination of Uwe Rosenberg’s work.

Overview

Ora et Labora—Latin for “pray and work”—is a game about medieval monastic economics. Uwe truly loves medieval settings. Medieval monasteries naturally included prayer, but even more of their life consisted of hard labor inside and outside the monastery. Each player manages a monastery, sending a prior and two lay brothers to work in buildings that produce goods. At the beginning, each player has only a 2 × 5 heartland board, but may purchase additional landscapes as the game progresses. Play pauses five times, and after each pause players can build settlements, ideally placing them where the surrounding settlement values are high. Ora et Labora offers two variants, France and Ireland, and players choose which one to use.


A handmade clergyman created by an overseas geek

Setup

  1. Production wheel and hand: choose the production wheel appropriate to the player count—three players; four players; the three- or four-player short game; or one or two players—and the correct side of the wheel hand. Use the slower-growth side for two players and the other side otherwise.
  2. Buildings: first decide whether to use the France or Ireland variant.
    • In a three-player game, remove buildings marked 4 in the lower-right. In a two-player game, also remove those marked 3+.
    • Turn the buildings to their France or Ireland side.
    • Sort buildings by the symbol at the center-left: a book or the letters A, B, C, and D. Display the book buildings on the table and place each other group beside the matching space on the wheel.
  3. Place the goods indicators on space 0 of the wheel and the settlement indicator on A.
  4. Stone and grapes: place the stone and grape indicators outside their corresponding wheel spaces. They are unavailable at the start and enter later. The Ireland variant has no grapes.
  5. Each player receives:
    • 1 heartland board
    • 3 clergy
    • 8 settlement cards
    • 1 reference card
    • 1 of each basic good: clay, wood, peat, coin, grain, and livestock
  6. Landscapes: sort both the long districts and square plots by the price in the lower-right, cheapest first, to form two stacks. Players must purchase the cheapest available tile of either type.

Game Flow

Only the three- and four-player rules are described here. The one- and two-player and short-game rules differ slightly. Once the Chinese edition is released, players who understand the three- and four-player game should be able to read the other rules quickly.

At the beginning, choose a starting player. That player takes the Starting Player marker and turns its one-coin side face up. The marker passes clockwise after every round.

Round Sequence

Each round contains five phases, resolved in order:

  1. At the beginning of the round, every player checks whether all three clergy are on buildings. If so, return all of them.
  2. Move the wheel hand one space counterclockwise, increasing the production of every good. If an indicator is already on the final space, the hand pushes it forward, so its production stays unchanged. In the France variant, grapes enter in Round 8 and stone in Round 13. Place their indicators on 0; at that point, the joker indicator may immediately be used to obtain stone or grapes.
  3. Whenever the hand passes a settlement-phase letter—A through E—pause play and insert a Settlement Phase, during which players build settlements.
  4. Beginning with the starting player and proceeding clockwise, each player takes one action. The starting player then takes one additional action.
  5. At the end of the round, pass the Starting Player marker clockwise.

Action Options

Choose one of the following three options as your action.

  • Place clergy: place one available clergyman—not currently on a board—either the prior or a lay brother, on an unused building and resolve its function.
    • You may place your own or another player’s clergyman. To use another player’s, pay that player 1 coin, increasing to 2 after the Whiskey Distillery in the Ireland variant or Winery in the France variant has been built. Alternatively, return one wine in France or whiskey in Ireland to the common supply as payment. In that case the other player receives nothing; the alcohol was a gift, which they happily drank on the spot.
    • You may place clergy without using the building’s function.
  • Fell trees or cut peat: remove one Forest or Moor card from your board, then take wood or peat equal to the number shown beside that good’s indicator on the production wheel.
    • This action uses no clergyman.
    • You may use the joker indicator to gain wood or peat.
    • You may take this action even if you have no Forest or Moor, and even if the indicator’s value is 0.
    • Removing a Forest or Moor exposes a landscape space on which you may later construct a building.
  • Construct a building: choose a currently displayed building, pay the resources in its upper-left corner, and place it on an empty space of your board.
    • Yellow monastery buildings must be adjacent to an existing yellow building.
    • The placement must satisfy the terrain requirement in the upper-left.
    • Buildings cannot be moved after construction.
    • If your prior is available, you may immediately place him on the newly constructed building and use its ability.

Bonus actions may be taken any number of times at any point during your turn.

  • Turn grain into straw: flip any number of grain goods to their straw side. You cannot reverse the conversion.
  • Exchange money: exchange five one-coin goods for one five-coin good or vice versa. You may also exchange wine for one coin or whiskey for two coins.
  • Purchase a landscape: during each turn or Settlement Phase, you may purchase one landscape. Pay the coins shown in the lower-right, choose a side, and place it. Long districts may extend only above or below your heartland. A square plot’s coast side may extend only at the upper or lower left of your heartland, while its mountain side may extend only at the upper or lower right. The tile must be adjacent to a landscape you already own.

Production Wheel

When a player gains resources from the production wheel through certain buildings, take the number of goods shown by the corresponding indicator—or the joker—then move that indicator to 0.

Settlement Phase

Whenever the wheel hand passes A, B, C, D, or E, insert a Settlement Phase. Players may each purchase one landscape at this time, proceeding clockwise from the starting player if more than one wishes to do so.

  1. Move the settlement indicator to the next letter.
  2. Each player may choose one settlement from their hand to build. Pay the food and energy in its upper-left corner, then place it on an empty space of your board.
    • Excess food and energy are wasted.
    • Observe the settlement’s terrain requirement.
    • Settlements cannot be moved.
    • You may construct only a settlement from before the current phase. In the first Settlement Phase, for example, you may build only one of the four book settlements; in the second, A settlements become available.
    • You may decline to construct a settlement.
  3. Add the buildings corresponding to the letter just passed to the display.

Bonus Round in a Three- or Four-Player Game

Once the hand passes E on its second circuit—Round 25—the game enters its final Bonus Round.

Each player returns their prior, then takes one bonus action in player order. That action may be constructing a building, after which the prior may be placed on it to use it, or activating any player’s building without paying a fee and regardless of whether it has already been used.

Scoring

Add the following three categories. The player with the most points wins!

  1. Goods value: add the values inside shields on goods. Alcohol is both valuable and worth points; for example, one wine plus four coins can count as 2 points.
  2. Building and settlement value: add the values inside shields.
  3. Settlement value: each settlement scores the sum of the small-house values on itself and the four buildings or settlements directly above, below, left, and right of it.

Review

As I said at the beginning, Ora et Labora is titanxxh’s favorite game. In my view, its design—from mechanisms to details—represents the peak of Uwe Rosenberg’s work. Rather than repeat myself, I will quote a passage from an earlier article:

After two quiet years, Uwe returned to the fair with a new work: Ora et Labora, titanxxh’s personal favorite, which my friends and I usually shorten to “Ora.” If I had to summarize its design in one word, it would be elegance. When introducing the game to newcomers unfamiliar with Uwe’s earlier work, they may regard its small wheel with curiosity or even confusion. Veteran Uwe players, however, can only marvel at the brilliance of the design. The circle truly is a perfect form. Just as people become more tactful after being shaped by experience, this wheel seems to have been refined across many years of Uwe’s work until it became such an elegant solution: nudge the hand, and everything is done. Tzolk’in later borrowed something of this idea. The design eliminates the tedious work of replenishing resources in Agricola, combines it with the timing function of the rondel in Le Havre, and makes introducing new resources during play extremely easy. The wheel naturally has limitations—for example, resources of the same type can grow at different rates in Agricola, which is impossible here—but that does not keep us from praising its elegance. Beyond substantially improving resource growth, Uwe introduced the relative position of buildings into Ora et Labora, transforming scoring and deepening the layers of thought required. Players must decide not only when to purchase which building, but also how to arrange their buildings to increase settlement scores. That demands stronger global strategy. Perhaps because this increased complexity, Uwe eliminated all randomness and controlled the number of every building to reduce the burden on players. The familiar feeding phase from Uwe’s earlier games also disappeared, replaced by a subtler and more flexible Settlement Phase. Players no longer suffer frustration from failing to feed their people. When building settlements, they have choices: even if they temporarily cannot construct a high-value settlement, they can build a slightly lower-scoring one, save resources, and prepare earlier for later stages. Because most settlement scoring comes from surrounding buildings, the settlement itself does not overly affect the final score, making the design friendlier. Another strength is player interaction. If another player constructs a building before you, that is not necessarily bad: you can still use it, perhaps even before its owner benefits, while saving the resources required to build it. On the other hand, doing so accelerates your opponent’s clergy cycle and slows your own. Finally, I believe the game’s greatest triumph is its kindness to trailing players. In many games, a poor early development—perhaps a key building is taken or a strategy disrupted—effectively removes someone from contention. Here, until the final stage, you still have opportunities to allocate resources sensibly, convert them into points, and catch up. For all these reasons, Ora et Labora became titanxxh’s favorite game, the one I have played most often by far, and one of the very few I rated a perfect 10 on BGG. A game can earn a high score for novelty, polished mechanisms, an enjoyable experience, and many other reasons. But if a player awards a perfect score, I think there is only one reason: profound love. If loving the right person at the right time is happiness, then meeting the right people and playing the right game at the right time must be another kind of happiness.
