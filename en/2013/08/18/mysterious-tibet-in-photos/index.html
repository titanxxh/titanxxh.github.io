Mysterious Tibet in Photos

titanxxh2013-08-182026-08-22

Jokhang Temple, Potala Palace, Mila Pass, Niyang River, Basum Tso, Lulang Forest, Nanyigou Valley, Namcha Barwa, Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon, Kadinggou Heavenly Buddha Waterfall, Yamdrok Lake, Karola Glacier, Duoqing Co, Mount Everest Base Camp, Yangbajain Hot Springs, and Namtso.

The beautiful scenery needs no introduction. Beyond that, my strongest impression of this journey through Tibet can be summarized in one sentence: the quality of the tourist infrastructure does not match the beauty of the landscape. From hotels to restaurants, the services fall far short of what a tourist destination should offer, yet prices are the same as in other tourist cities—and sometimes even higher.


Our itinerary, covering nearly 3,000 kilometers in total

Day 1: Jokhang Temple & Potala Palace

Our guide said that three kinds of people especially love Tibet: those who enjoy the meeting and exchange of different cultures, those with religious faith, and those searching for spiritual refuge.


Pilgrims bring the prayers of all living beings to Jokhang Temple and complete one hundred thousand full-body prostrations before entering. Their faces are the darkest and their clothes the dirtiest, but their hearts are the purest.

Lhasa is the center of Tibet, but Jokhang Temple is the true heart of Lhasa. The Tibetan Empire was imagined as a supine demoness, with Jokhang Temple standing over her heart to subdue her. The site was originally a lake, so building the temple first required filling it. Goats were the principal means of transport, carrying bags of earth and sand on their backs until the lake had been filled and the temple's foundation could be laid. The modern name Lhasa actually evolved from the Jokhang Temple. The city was not originally called LASA; old documents call it RASA. RA means goat and SA means earth, so the name meant “the place built by goats.” Later, after this sacred Buddhist hall was erected—with an image of the Buddha, scriptures, stupas, and worshippers arriving from every direction—people regarded it as the land of the Buddha and changed the name to Lhasa. LA means Buddha in Tibetan, and SA means land.


The golden roof of Jokhang Temple, bright enough to dazzle your eyes!


Unlike temples in central China, where incense is burned, Tibetan temples use butter lamps that remain continuously lit.

Like many temples, Jokhang does not permit photography inside. What makes it different is that pilgrims and tourists follow separate paths. Ordinary visitors cannot enter every inner chapel, which means they also cannot see the only surviving original life-size statue of the twelve-year-old Shakyamuni Buddha.


A distant view of Potala Palace from Jokhang Temple: the White Palace below and the Red Palace above

Built against the mountainside, Potala Palace is magnificent: a miracle of architectural history, a museum of the past, and a palace of art. Sixty percent of Tibet's treasures are said to be gathered here. It holds untold quantities of gold, jewels, thangkas, murals, and scriptures, along with the stupas of successive Dalai Lamas. The stupa of the Fifth Dalai Lama is the largest, sheathed in gold and set with innumerable gems. One of them is said to be a luminous pearl taken from an elephant's brain and to be beyond price.


The White Palace’s exterior is not covered with modern paint. It is coated with a mixture of yak milk, honey, Tibetan medicine, and a little lime. During renovations every five to eight years, the mixture is poured from above, producing this appearance.


To reduce the weight of the Red Palace, these “walls” are made from bundles of red branches. They reportedly take five years to prepare. When replaced, they are first fumigated with a Tibetan medicinal smoke and then changed one bundle at a time.


A thangka of the Four Heavenly Kings. This is also the last place where photography is allowed. Their pupils are painted in the final stroke—the literal finishing touch. PS: Their eyes are enormous.


Tibet’s Shoton Festival begins in two days, and these decorations hang from all the streetlights. Everyone drinks yogurt during the festival, and a giant Buddha thangka is unveiled on the first day.

 

Day 2: Mila Pass & Basum Tso

![](https://titanxxh-1259211834.cos.ap-shanghai.myqcloud.com/TenDaysInTibet/IMG_5469_zps8cff96e2.jpg) The first ray of morning sunlight in Lhasa


Many people had clearly set out early. The birthplace of the Tibetan king Songtsen Gampo was crowded with photographers. Tibetan mastiffs and yaks stood nearby for photographs, but the moment you pressed the shutter, somebody appeared to demand payment. What a rip-off.


A typical Tibetan tent woven from yak hair. In rain, the fibers’ pores first become clogged with water and then keep more rain out. Yak hair is also said to protect against lightning strikes.


Although the mountains are bare, the blue sky and white clouds give them an imposing grandeur.


Cyclists from Xi’an Jiaotong University. The stone behind them marks the altitude as 5,013 meters. To be honest, when I got out and walked, I felt almost weightless.


A bridge reaching the opposite bank


“A steadfast pillar amid the current, dividing the waters in silent contemplation”—the phrase describes this great rock standing in the river.


“Lovely spring is no match for a dream, where fragrant grass grows; carry your dreams with you through blue skies, white clouds, green hills and clear water, while a gentle breeze crosses the setting sun.”


A dam—or perhaps a hydroelectric station?


Basum Tso means “green water” in Tibetan. After seeing the lakes later in the journey, this one seemed less remarkable. PS: tso means lake.


A peach tree embracing a pine


Nyingchi lies at a lower altitude and has the strongest economy in Tibet. If nothing else, its hotels are much better than those in Lhasa. PS: Flying into Nyingchi and then traveling gradually westward would probably be a sensible itinerary.

Day 3: Lulang Forest & the Great Cypress

![](https://titanxxh-1259211834.cos.ap-shanghai.myqcloud.com/TenDaysInTibet/IMG_5792_zps85a20da4.jpg) We had been traveling on mountain roads, where trees usually grow on only one side. A road shaded by trees on both sides is uncommon, and the trees here are not normally this tall.


A tilted photograph of the forest


Namcha Barwa, whose name means “spear piercing the sky,” has been rated the most beautiful mountain in China. It is the fifteenth-highest mountain in the world; the fourteen above it all exceed 8,000 meters. Clouds and mist surround it throughout the year, but today’s good weather revealed most of the summit. The best viewpoint is actually not Lulang Forest but the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon, where the mountain is nearest, the vertical difference greatest, and the sight most overwhelming. Seeing it from there requires extraordinary luck.

A poet once sighed: compared with Everest's solid mass, I am more enchanted by Namcha Barwa's graceful, upright cliffs soaring into the clouds; compared with Shishapangma's desolation and dryness, I prefer Namcha Barwa's organic union of swaying greenery with snow and ice; compared with Mount Kailash's isolation from the world, I feel more attached to the shifting clouds, movement, and vitality among Namcha Barwa's slopes. This may be the most perfect expression of the mountain's character.


Stone-pot chicken, Lulang’s signature dish. The pot is carved from stone from Mêdog; it does not crack when heated, and food tastes better the longer the pot is used.


The King of Cypress Trees, said to be 2,600 years old

Day 4: Nanyigou Valley & Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon

![](https://titanxxh-1259211834.cos.ap-shanghai.myqcloud.com/TenDaysInTibet/IMG_6032_zps6cf6d892.jpg) After reaching Nanyigou Valley, we first visited a Lhoba home. The Lhoba were the last ethnic minority officially recognized in China. When they were encountered, they still practiced slash-and-burn agriculture and then moved directly into socialist society.


A puppy followed our sightseeing vehicle for nearly a kilometer simply to beg for a bite of meat.


I wonder whether anyone lives in this thatched hut.


Love Stronger Than Stone: an enormous boulder split in two, with a peach tree growing from the crack. This remarkable sight inspired a local love story about the daughter of the Peach Tree King and a man from the tribe. When spring winds soften into rain, the blossoms within the giant rock sway in the breeze, revealing love’s miraculous power.


The Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon is 504.6 kilometers long, reaches a maximum depth of 6,009 meters, and averages 2,268 meters deep. It is the world’s largest canyon, filled with rapids and dangerous shoals as the river roars through it.

The day we visited was rainy and overcast, so we missed the chance to see Namcha Barwa up close from the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon. Then again, even on a clear day it might remain hidden.


A horse startled by our tour bus and fleeing without knowing where to go


The drizzle had just stopped as sunlight began to appear.

Day 5: Kadinggou Valley

That day's route took us from Nyingchi back to Lhasa, passing the Heavenly Buddha Waterfall in Kadinggou Valley.


A distant view of the magnificent waterfall descending from the heavens


A single-log bridge. The sign says no more than three people may cross at once, but nobody seems to pay attention.


Standing directly beneath the waterfall, with spray striking my face and fresh air filling my lungs, all the exhaustion of climbing disappeared.


A little plump is the ideal figure.


A path made from tree-stump slices. Why does their color make them look like pieces of sausage?


Back in Lhasa, today was the first day of the Shoton Festival. Worshippers poured into the holy city and caused traffic jams.

Day 6: Yamdrok Lake & Karola Glacier

From today onward, our Tibetan journey reached its climax. The scenery before this had merely been the appetizers; we had to save room for the main course.


When a landscape enters the heart, even a foreign place becomes home.


Does it seem as though we are level with the clouds? PS: This photograph feels like a car advertisement.


Yamdrok Tso, usually called Yamdrok Lake, lies less than 100 kilometers from Lhasa. Together with Namtso and Lake Manasarovar, it is one of Tibet’s three sacred lakes. It is the largest inland lake on the northern side of the Himalayas, and its combination of water and mountains is unmatched in southern Tibet. Yamdrok is enormous and divided into several smaller lakes; no viewpoint reveals the whole of it.


“I want to fly higher.”


Snow-covered mountains on both sides of Yamdrok Lake


Karola Glacier stands more than 5,600 meters above sea level, the highest point on this journey. Glacier and white clouds merge into one. PS: Because of global warming, the snow line retreats a considerable distance every year. I do not know whether the glacier will still be visible many years from now.


We stayed in Gyantse that day. This is the Gyantse Dzong fortress. In 1904, British forces advanced on Gyantse and fiercely attacked the fortress. Chinese soldiers and civilians held their position for three months and repelled repeated assaults. Only after their ammunition and reinforcements were exhausted, leaving them hopelessly outnumbered, did the fortress fall.

Day 7: Duoqing Co

![](https://titanxxh-1259211834.cos.ap-shanghai.myqcloud.com/TenDaysInTibet/IMG_6778_zpsde21b247.jpg) Because our driver's Mandarin pronunciation was imperfect, we kept thinking the lake was called “Affectionate Lake,” a homophone of Duoqing Co. On the drive there, my mother became so excited photographing herds of horses, cattle, and sheep that she even dropped her phone. The driver said we felt far too much affection for this land.


A herd of cattle


A flock of sheep


A cracked road runs toward the horizon.


A panorama of Duoqing Co. Ordinary travel agencies rarely recommend this place, mainly because it is so remote; continue beyond it and you reach Nepal. Yet the scenery here is at least as beautiful as that of Ranwu Lake, which we had originally planned to visit.


A breeze began to rise. The water was no longer still and the reflection no longer sharp, but its blurred outline created a different kind of hazy beauty.


A sea of rapeseed flowers beside the road


Tashilhunpo Monastery is the largest monastery in the Shigatse region of Tibet. It stands on the eastern slope of Mount Nyima, west of Shigatse city. Since the Fourth Panchen Lama, it has served as the seat of successive Panchen Lamas and is comparable to the Dalai Lama’s Potala Palace. It is the leading tourist attraction in Tsang.

Day 8: Mount Everest Base Camp

This stop was not part of our original itinerary, mainly because we worried that my aunts would not be physically able to manage it. After several days of acclimatization, however, they unexpectedly asked to visit Everest themselves. We rose very early and spent seventeen hours on the road, finally returning to Shigatse just before midnight.


Beneath the morning glow, only Everest’s pointed summit appeared.


A winding mountain road. The final hundred-plus kilometers were all gravel like this, and reaching base camp took four hours.


Vegetation was even scarcer here, and the mountains were completely bare.


Bharal, or Himalayan blue sheep. They can eat only the grass growing between rocks, and every one of them is thin.


The best place to view Everest is not base camp, but a bend in the road shortly before reaching it.


This is base camp: essentially a ring of tents and several vehicles. Conditions are harsh, and fortunately we did not plan to spend the night.


“I came to your city and walked the road you once traveled.”


Rongbuk Monastery, the highest monastery in the world

Days 9–10: Yangbajain Hot Springs & Namtso

![](https://titanxxh-1259211834.cos.ap-shanghai.myqcloud.com/TenDaysInTibet/IMG_7193_zpse6b6f23b.jpg) Clouds hung like ribbons halfway up the mountains.


Yangbajain is a natural geothermal hot spring. Because the water is too hot, it must cool naturally through two pools before visitors can use it. PS: If you want to bathe here, I recommend spending one night at this location, because hotel guests can use the springs for free. Imagine soaking in steaming water beneath a starry sky at night—how delightful that would be. Watching snow fall in winter would be more poetic still.


Words left by passing travelers inside a temporary prefabricated building at Namtso


Namtso means “heavenly lake” in both Tibetan and Mongolian. The azure sky and blue lake merge into one, with the main peak of the Nyenchen Tanglha range visible in the distance.


The evening glow and a lone bird fly together.


Autumn water and the endless sky share a single hue.


Meditation beneath the moonlight


“Across the starry Milky Way, can I meet you? Distance does not frighten me; I only wish I could race to your side this instant.” The stars above Namtso. Many people saw a meteor cross the sky that night.


The sun rises in the east.


“Alone—just me, walking proudly; body and spirit leap as I gaze toward the heavens and embrace the kiss of stars and moon.”


All kinds of admission tickets; some can no longer be found.
