From Boy to Father, in Just a Few World Cups

titanxxh2022-11-212026-08-22

Childhood

I was a late-blooming boy, and my memories from before middle school are hazy. Moving to Shanghai for middle school in 2002 made the divide from my childhood feel even sharper. Yet one thread has remained crystal clear from beginning to end: football.

I was in primary school when enthusiasm for football in China was at its height. After class, my friends and I headed straight to the pitch. My mother enrolled me in football lessons, and even after getting heatstroke I still insisted on going. I repeatedly scraped my knees until the wounds became infected, but I kept playing. I was hopeless at Chinese class, yet I once played football alone at home and wrote a little story about a match between my left foot and my right. It was even published in a newspaper.

The 2002 World Cup in Korea and Japan was the first World Cup I remember. China had reached the finals for the first time, and schools suspended classes so everyone could watch. We all know what happened afterward: what I thought was the beginning of a glorious era for China turned out to be its peak.

That was also when I found my national team—Brazil. The three Rs were unstoppable, and Brazil won all seven matches to claim its fifth title. It is no exaggeration to say that the city was awash with yellow No. 9 shirts—quite literally. Ronaldo won everyone over. Even today, he remains the only football star I have ever truly followed.

After moving to Shanghai, I suddenly lost the environment in which I had played: none of my new classmates played football. I was still at school and rarely had a chance to watch television, so I followed the game through Soccer Weekly and programs such as Football Night and Total Soccer. In today’s language, I gradually became an armchair fan.

Back in 2002, I had been a child in a small city with very few sources of information and knew little about the players. The magazines and television programs opened up a much wider football world. I learned about Ronaldo’s extraordinary story—how repeated, devastating injuries had kept him out of almost every match in the preceding years; how few people fancied Brazil before the tournament; and how almost nobody expected him to score eight goals at a single World Cup.

The 2006 World Cup in Germany arrived just after my high-school entrance exams, so I could watch without restraint. Brazil’s squad looked invincible: the magical quartet of Ronaldo, Ronaldinho, Kaká and Adriano, backed by recent Copa América and Confederations Cup triumphs. Everyone expected Brazil to reach a fourth consecutive World Cup final, lift the trophy for a sixth time, and put Ronaldo alongside Pelé.

Instead, Brazil fell to France once again. I still remember sitting alone in my small room, miserable for a long time. It was the first—and only—time football made me cry. What I could not have imagined was that the match would also be Ronaldo’s last for Brazil.

As Ronaldo put on weight, his career entered an irreversible decline. People lamented the collective early decline of Brazil’s gifted generation and condemned the players for their lack of discipline. I was a hot-blooded teenager and, like plenty of kids today, loved arguing with strangers on Tieba. I would not admit it aloud, but the truth was unavoidable: Brazil was producing fewer and fewer exceptional players. The center of world football had shifted decisively to Europe, the Champions League mattered more than ever, and every player was expected to adopt a European way of playing.

University

At the 2010 World Cup in South Africa, Brazil’s centerpiece was Kaká, whose style was thoroughly European, while the manager was the stubborn Dunga. Dunga had led Brazil to the Confederations Cup the year before. I still remember how thrilled I was when Brazil came back from two goals down to score three in the final. Overall, though, Dunga’s relentlessly pragmatic football was difficult to love. He also left Neymar—already famous in Brazil—out of the squad, for which I cursed him in my head ten thousand times.

This team, caught awkwardly between Brazilian and European football, also went out in the quarterfinals. As four years earlier, the match made no sense. Brazil dominated the Netherlands in the first half, and Felipe Melo produced a sublime through ball for the opening goal. In the second half he became the villain: an own goal and a goal from a corner sent Brazil home, and Dunga was dismissed soon afterward.

Dunga’s stubborn team selection denied young players enough opportunities to develop, helping create a long talent drought in Brazilian football. Brazil lost to Paraguay at the first knockout hurdle of the 2011 Copa América and sank to its lowest point, but after years of watching football I could accept it calmly.

By the 2013 Confederations Cup, Brazil qualified only as the host, and even I had little confidence that this young Seleção could win. Once again, the team proved me spectacularly wrong. In hindsight, however, the 3–0 demolition of a declining Spain in the final may only have reinforced returning manager Luiz Felipe Scolari’s faith in an outdated tactical system.

University gave me more freedom, so this was the period when I watched the most football. The head-to-head brilliance of Messi and Ronaldo was compelling, of course. Yet perhaps because I supported Brazil, the more matches I watched, the harder it became to recover my old excitement.

Then Neymar arrived in Europe. His unmistakably Brazilian style was exhilarating: he dared to try things on the pitch, even if that naturally earned him plenty of “special attention” from defenders. Europeans did not necessarily appreciate that style, but so what? Is that not precisely what Brazil supporters come to see?

The university network made PT downloads extremely fast. I joined one tracker after another and gradually began collecting recordings of Brazil matches from years gone by. By the eve of the 2014 World Cup, I had found recordings of almost every Brazil match at every major tournament since the 1994 World Cup. Most people on the Brazil supporters’ forum probably downloaded something I shared.

At the 2014 World Cup in Brazil, every Brazil supporter was eager to see how Neymar would perform in his first World Cup. To be honest, my expectations were modest. Then he scored four goals in the group stage, and Brazil even made it past the quarterfinals.

Neymar and captain Thiago Silva were both unavailable for the semifinal against Germany, but I still thought Brazil had a chance if they played conservatively. By the time it was 5–0 in the first half, I was so angry that I slammed the table and injured my little finger. It took me a long time to recover from that match. I stopped collecting match recordings too.

Once I began working, my free time shrank dramatically and I had to pare down my hobbies. Staying up at night for football was an extravagant one, especially with the ungodly kickoff times in La Liga and the Champions League. I could manage a few Premier League matches or find a replay the following day.

I watched quite a few of the dozen or so matches in which Neymar led Barcelona while Messi was injured. He looked destined for greatness, equally capable with or without the ball. Sadly, Neymar’s most dazzling peak never coincided with the best version of Brazil. After six matches in South American qualifying for the 2018 World Cup, Brazil had played dreadful football and, if I remember correctly, won only once. Fortunately, Tite took charge, produced a long winning streak, and secured qualification early.

Working Life

The 2018 World Cup in Russia was the closest I have ever come to the tournament: I was there in person, together with the person I love most. (See my 2018 World Cup in Russia vlog.)

The moment FIFA opened ticket sales—before the group draw had even taken place—I blindly bought a semifinal ticket. Rationally, I chose the wrong half of the bracket: the host was fixed as A1, so the probability of another seeded team landing in the same half was only 3/8. I later secured a ticket to the third-place match as well and arranged a long holiday far in advance. When the draw was finally made, my semifinal turned out to be in Brazil’s half of the bracket. I was ecstatic.

I had already put on my Brazil shirt before boarding the flight to Russia. Then Brazil were eliminated almost as soon as I landed. My phone did not even have a signal when the young man behind me announced that Belgium had knocked Brazil out. I was devastated; Russia would simply have to become a holiday.

To be fair, Russia’s combination of ruggedness and delicacy was captivating, and prices were low. It was well worth visiting. For the World Cup final, we walked an extraordinarily long way to reach the Fan Fest at Moscow State University. Packed shoulder to shoulder in the square with a crowd of strongly scented European gentlemen, we watched the entire match. All I remember is that the instant the final ended, the heavens opened. The resulting aroma was truly unforgettable.

Becoming a Father

The year 2020 carried a special significance. The beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic transformed everyone’s life, while the birth of Molly—our little Youzi—made me a father. Nobody could have foreseen the timeline the world would enter after 2020. Looking back, I feel fortunate that my wife and I visited Russia in 2018, followed by Spain and Portugal in 2019.

After Molly arrived, Dad’s free time dropped all the way to zero. In that case, I decided to hand my unfinished football career over to her. We gave her a ball to play with when she was tiny. Her gross motor development was well ahead of schedule, and she learned to walk, run and kick a ball very early. A broad lawn downstairs gave us the perfect place to run around. Whenever her mother saw other little girls playing on the grass in dresses, she would tease us for raising ours like a boy.

Shanghai spent several months under lockdown in 2022. If we could not travel to Qatar, we would watch from home—but we still had to create a proper World Cup atmosphere. I ordered Brazil’s new 2022–23 shirt and the children’s version from Nike well in advance. I have to say, a whole family walking down the street in matching Brazil shirts looks exceptionally cool.

In twenty years, Brazil fell from its pedestal, dropped out of the world’s top ten at its lowest point, rose again, and returned to No. 1.

In twenty years, one generation’s footballing idol retired, while the Brazilian children who grew up watching him became idols to a new generation of Brazil players.

In twenty years, the little boy who played football on muddy ground every day became a father running across the grass with a little girl.

Looking back, twenty years amount to only five World Cups. As long as the passion for the game remains, so does the little boy within.
