7 Wonders Duel with All Expansions: Embracing Uncertainty

titanxxh2024-11-092026-08-22

Introduction

The Lord of the Rings: Duel for Middle-earth was released recently. After trying it a few times, I felt that it lacked a little of its predecessor’s flavor. It may be more suitable for playing with children: every missing resource costs only one coin, reducing the arithmetic.

So instead of chasing the trend and recommending Duel for Middle-earth, I still prefer its predecessor and what I consider the best light strategy game for two players: 7 Wonders Duel, with both expansions.

That is not merely my personal enthusiasm. If BGG’s rankings are filtered to two-player games, the only title ahead of it is a former overall number one, Twilight Struggle.

Someone may ask whether discussing a game almost ten years after release is rather late. There is another important reason: I have recently played it constantly on Board Game Arena, and my Elo reached a new high of 652, number eleven in the world. I feel that my understanding has risen another level. I previously made several game-analysis videos on Bilibili, but looking back now, much of my thinking at the time was mistaken.

Only 7 Wonders Duel with Both Expansions Is the Real 7 Wonders Duel

Simply look at the empty slots left around the game board and you can tell that everything was designed together. The publisher shamelessly split it up for separate sale, the same drip-feed strategy as video-game DLC—though this particular drip tastes excellent.

In my view, adding both expansions transforms the original game completely. In the base game, any opponent with a little experience will usually drag the contest into final scoring, making the overall experience rather dry. To play well, you can do little beyond improving the precision of your calculations for a tiny advantage.

The first expansion, Pantheon, introduces gods in Age II. Gods bring many powerful effects, while players can partly control which gods will appear during Age I. This further increases the importance of planning in the first age and gives the game greater cohesion.

The second expansion, Agora, introduces the Senate and conspiracy systems. The Senate greatly increases the early value of blue cards and enriches the choices in Age I. Conspiracies add hidden information. Because some conspiracy cards are extremely powerful, using them well can accelerate a player’s path to victory dramatically. With a new win condition—political supremacy—many games no longer reach final scoring, and tension throughout play rises enormously.

For those reasons, many highly ranked Elo players use both expansions, and plenty, like me, play nothing else. I must complain briefly about BGA’s Arena design. Each season’s format is decided by the group that played the most during the previous season, so players who grind only Arena always vote for vanilla. I am not alone in criticizing this: the world’s top-ranked player, frankenstain, complains about it constantly too.

The expansions appear to increase randomness. Does that mean they reduce strategic depth? I do not believe so. Conspiracies decrease predictability and the value of exact calculation, but require players to understand the broader position more deeply. When behind, conspiracies provide chances to reverse the game. Put differently, a player who is ahead must know how to prevent an opponent’s conspiracy from stealing victory. Frankenstain’s record provides evidence: his win rate against nearly every other elite player is at least seventy percent.

What Are We Playing When We Play 7 Wonders Duel?

Everything below concerns games with both expansions.

The first task is understanding how the game is won. The rules provide four victory conditions: military, scientific, political, and civilian, meaning victory on points. Those conditions are not equally balanced. As BGA premium members, we can view everyone’s statistics:

frankenstain: political 28%, military 16%, scientific 11%, civilian 18%. The remaining percentage consists of losses.

Me: political 24%, military 12%, scientific 6%, civilian 20%.

Ninety percent of my 292 games used both expansions. Most of frankenstain’s probably did as well, though I am not certain.

Political victories occur most often, while final scoring is relatively uncommon. Our first conclusion is therefore that most games end early, and political supremacy is the easiest route.

This makes intuitive sense because the game’s victory paths are not orthogonal. Advancing the military track also supports the struggle for the Senate: military progress always contributes toward political victory, while political progress does not necessarily contribute toward military victory. Political victories are therefore most common. Political supremacy can even be achieved in Age I. Scientific supremacy, by contrast, is possible only in Age III, and military supremacy generally also requires the third age.

The routes to victory differ enormously. Because the starting wonders are drafted and each game’s setup changes, the two players may be trying to accomplish entirely different things. In that sense, this is fundamentally one of today’s popular asymmetric competitive games. Compared with rules that distinguish the two sides from the outset, 7 Wonders Duel’s approach feels more elegant.

Once this is understood, another common beginner’s error becomes clear: trying to take everything and ultimately failing on every front.

How Will I Win?

After understanding how the game can be won, the next task is identifying how you personally will win this particular game. Once you know that, you largely understand how you could lose too. The two are not perfectly symmetrical because conspiracy cards exist.

We need to understand the typical route for each victory condition:

  1. Political supremacy

Political supremacy requires you to outnumber your opponent in all six Senate chambers. In other words, you need at least six more influence cubes in total. Cubes come from many sources:

  • When you own two or four blue cards, every white Senate card you acquire places two or three cubes. If your opponent has only one blue card or none, they place only one, creating the difference: +1/+2/+3.
  • Controlling the blue +2 Senate chamber means your blue-card count is treated as two higher. In practice, each white card will usually place one additional cube: +1.
  • A black conspiracy card places one cube directly: +1.
  • One conspiracy places one of your cubes, removes one opposing cube, and moves one of yours: +2.
  • Advancing the military track lets you place a cube. Advancing again can remove an opposing cube and move one of yours: +1.
  • The god Neptune can remove one military penalty token and resolve its effect: +1.
  • Selecting the wonder Knossos lets you place one cube immediately; building it lets you place one and move another: +1 and +1.

Clearly, reaching six more cubes than the opponent is easy. Because many conspiracies also move a cube, rearranging your influence is not difficult.

A typical political route is therefore to build two blue cards early in Age I, then acquire two white cards and control four Senate chambers. With further help from the military track or Neptune, fill the remaining chambers.

Money is the most important resource for political supremacy. The worst possible situation is for an opponent to reveal a white card when you cannot afford it. Avoid that as much as possible. Some resource shortages can instead be covered through chamber effects.

Political supremacy can generally be achieved in Age II. Even when it is not, controlling more Senate chambers provides many bonuses and often yields a substantial advantage, especially after entering Age III with chambers that pay money for red, yellow, or blue cards.

  1. Military supremacy

Military supremacy requires pushing the conflict pawn all the way to the opponent’s capital—in other words, gaining nine more military strength than the opponent.

  • Red cards in the three ages provide different amounts of military strength: +1/+2/+3.
  • Mars provides two: +2.
  • One conspiracy provides two: +2.
  • One conspiracy lets you construct any one card from the three, six, or nine cards excluded from the game: +?.
  • The Strategy progress token adds one military strength whenever you acquire a red card.
  • One Senate chamber adds one military strength while you control it: +1.
  • Several wonders provide one; the Colossus provides two: +1/+2.

Because three-strength red cards appear only in Age III, military supremacy usually cannot happen before then without Strategy. Once Age III begins, controlling which player reveals cards becomes the most important factor. Effects that change the reveal order, such as extra turns or gods that let a player pass without taking an age card, are therefore among the most valuable resources.

A typical military route is to build two or three two-strength red cards in Age II, keep a military wonder or a two-strength conspiracy in reserve, and wait for a chained finishing blow in Age III.

Military buildings themselves provide no effects, so a purely military strategy is inferior to Senate development. Military progress does provide one or two advantages in the Senate, however, so it often begins as a by-product of pursuing political supremacy and converts into military victory later.

  1. Scientific supremacy

Scientific supremacy requires six different scientific symbols.

  • Remember that Law counts as a symbol and is available both on a god card and as a progress token. In practice, seven symbols exist and you need six. Because two kinds appear only in Age III, scientific victory cannot occur before the third age. You generally need four symbols by the end of Age II, and must be careful that the opponent does not bury crucial cards beneath a wonder or conspiracy.
  • Age I contains two science cards costing two coins that chain into Age II cards with the same symbols.
  • Nisaba can copy one of the opponent’s symbols, but naturally requires the opponent to possess one.
  • One conspiracy swaps one of your green or blue cards with one belonging to your opponent and can assemble the sixth symbol.
  • The Mausoleum wonder can recover a discarded card.
  • Hades can recover a discarded card.
  • One conspiracy lets you build any card from the three, six, or nine excluded from the game.

The scientific route has major problems. First, it is incompatible with the other victory conditions. High-level players rarely begin with scientific supremacy as their first priority. Second, the available progress tokens may not be powerful enough to justify science as a side benefit. Third, the route places heavy demands on Age I: you may need to construct one or two science cards immediately, creating considerable economic pressure. Finally, the reveal order in Age III may not favor you.

A typical scientific victory therefore begins by building one or two science cards in Age I while maintaining an acceptable economy, surviving the opponent’s pressure in Age II and using Law to reach five symbols, then waiting for a chained finish in Age III—often while already on the edge of defeat.

Scientific supremacy is usually a comeback mechanism in a disadvantaged game, not the primary strategy. It may also be worth considering when the five face-up progress tokens include something especially powerful, making two science cards attractive in Age I.

  1. Civilian victory

Because civilian victory is determined by points, Age III—with the highest-scoring cards—is crucial. Major sources include:

  • Athena: 9 points.
  • The Great Pyramid: 9 points.
  • Temple cards: one is worth 5, two 12, and three 21. BGA does not display these points in real time.
  • Blue cards in Age III.
  • Yellow cards in Age III: a base of 3 points plus some money.
  • A progress token worth 7 points.
  • The military track, worth 2–10 points.

Other points are easy to forget:

  • If Astarte’s coins remain unspent, the god is effectively worth 7 points.
  • Senate chambers. The two central chambers are worth 3 points each, so compete for them whenever you have movement abilities.
  • One conspiracy swaps a green or blue card between players. Exchanging a three-point blue card for a seven-point one creates an eight-point swing.

There is no single typical route to civilian victory. It normally becomes relevant when, at the end of Age II, a player predicts that neither side can win early. It appears more often in dry games or when both players follow balanced, conservative plans. Frequently one player pursues an alternate victory while the other must defend and score simultaneously.

From a scoring perspective, most Age III cards do not differ dramatically in strength. If each side can take one normally, the gap stays small. The real danger comes when Age III reveals an unhelpful black card, or a blue card that your broken resource chain makes unaffordable. If you can control the reveal order to avoid those situations and keep your resources intact, you gain a large third-age advantage.

After analyzing how you will win a particular game, one repeated keyword is impossible to miss: revealing cards.

Control over reveals is the most important element in the game. Skilled players treat every reveal with extreme caution. If the card turned face up is exactly the decisive card their opponent needs, the work of the entire game may be wasted.

Anything That Can Go Wrong Will Go Wrong

Imagine these situations:

  • You reveal a three-strength military card for the opponent, who follows it with a two-strength conspiracy plus another three strength and destroys you instantly.
  • You have controlled the opponent’s access to stone perfectly, then reveal an Age II yellow wild resource card that solves their emergency.
  • Your opponent has no wonder capable of granting an extra turn. You reveal a science card whose symbol they already own; they take the Theology progress token, and suddenly all their wonders grant extra turns.

After experiencing these scenes repeatedly, you overturn the table in fury and shout, “Trash game. Nothing but luck.”

If you have read this far, however, you know that poor luck alone cannot explain everything. Otherwise frankenstain could never have reached an Elo of 888.

Those reveal results are frustrating because the worst outcome among all possibilities occurred, moving the game completely toward the opponent and destroying your advantage. It is Murphy’s law: “Anything that can go wrong will go wrong.”

We will not debate the philosophy here. Within this game, when such situations keep occurring, ask:

  • Why is it your turn to reveal a card right now?
  • If a reveal is unavoidable, why choose this card rather than another?
  • If this particular card must be revealed, how many possible results can you accept and how many are intolerable?

The first question is the most important. People remember bad results but overlook basic facts:

  • Is it possible that you simply reveal more cards than your opponent during a game and therefore encounter more bad events?
  • When you reveal a card, it usually means no good face-up card remains. Where did the good cards go?
  • When you do not want to reveal a card, your opponent often does not want to either. Can you use some method to force them to reveal?

Once that logic is understood, it becomes clear why reveals are the game’s central concern. If we assume that a reveal is unavoidable, we must answer the second and third questions:

  • Will revealing this card reverse the order of later reveals?
  • Is the card beneath it face down or face up? Is it black or white, or an ordinary age card?
  • Could you reveal a card that kills you immediately?
  • Might the opponent be unable to afford the revealed card?

Success often hides in details. This reminds me of tennis great Roger Federer’s commencement address at Dartmouth College. Federer played 1,526 singles matches and won 82 percent of them. Yet measured point by point, he won only 54 percent. His original lesson was that once you understand that you lose nearly one point for every two you win, you learn not to torture yourself over every shot but to play the next point well.

Let us view the issue from another angle. If every point has only a 54 percent chance of being won, what is the probability of winning a match under tennis rules? It can be calculated recursively. I also found a website, Probability and Tennis, whose data show:

With a 54 percent chance of winning each point, the probability of winning the entire match is an astonishing 91 percent! So, Mr. Federer, what happened in those other nine percent of matches? The explanation is straightforward: tennis uses elimination tournaments, opponents become stronger in later rounds, and Federer’s point-winning probability there was surely below 54 percent, causing his match-winning probability to fall sharply.

Question Uncertainty → Understand Uncertainty → Embrace Uncertainty

Finally, I want to explain why I particularly love 7 Wonders Duel. As they grow older, every board gamer must face the inevitable decline in time and energy available for games. Board games require people around you, and the friends who once played beside you often confront the same problem, reducing your number of sessions even further. I call this the board-gaming world’s Davis double kill. Games with the lowest possible player count—two—naturally become the last refuge for middle-aged players like us.

Limited energy and time also mean that a complex, lengthy game is difficult to study deeply. I am not very satisfied with the growing trend toward “mechanism stew” in modern board-game design. I prefer games with simple mechanisms, easy entry, and enough randomness that a beginner’s wild punches can sometimes defeat an old master.

My view of board-game design did not begin this way. It traveled through a complete cycle. Borrowing today’s popular phrasing—question X, understand X, become X—we might say: question uncertainty, understand uncertainty, embrace uncertainty.

When I first discovered board games, my taste favored highly deterministic designs: Uwe Rosenberg’s Ora et Labora, with zero randomness; the worker-placement ancestor Caylus, also with zero randomness; or the three-versus-three mode of Legends of the Three Kingdoms. Such deterministic games gave me an illusion: when I won, it was because I anticipated the enemy and calculated everything; when I lost, it was because I failed to think far enough. In competitive sport, weakness is the original sin.

That attitude may be connected to meritocracy throughout childhood, or what could be called a student’s mindset. For a small-town test taker, the world one experiences for a long time is often a deterministic environment constructed by adults after external interference has been screened away.

As I played more games, I gradually realized that, just as in school, someone is always stronger. In classic abstract games, no amount of time may let you defeat a Go master. What is the point of a game you always lose? Slowly, I understood the need for randomness. Many games introduce uncertainty precisely so that newcomers can experience the joy of victory within their first few plays. It resembles encountering a good opportunity, the right person, or a rising era in life: the road becomes much smoother.

Randomness creates hope, but sometimes it also inflicts brutal misfortune. Lose several games in succession and you may still erupt: “Trash game. All luck.”

Later, my understanding of uncertainty changed again. Like 7 Wonders Duel, Agricola has always been one of my favorite games. Whether Family Growth appears in round five or seven has a decisive and completely random effect on the position. After enough games, however, you notice that experts manage randomness extraordinarily well. Every game somehow seems to follow their rhythm.

7 Wonders Duel gave me a similar experience. Its overall weight is slightly lower than Agricola’s and lands exactly in my sweet spot. Meanwhile, BGA dramatically increased the number of games I could play, accelerating improvement. I defeated players rated above 700 even when my own rating was still low. The experience of a weaker player overcoming a stronger one greatly increased my affection, and gradually it became my favorite two-player game.

At the end of this article, reading the guide probably will not grant you mastery of 7 Wonders Duel. I hope, at least, that when you face uncertainty in life—good or bad—you can meet it with a calm heart.
