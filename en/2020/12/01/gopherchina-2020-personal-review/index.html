My GopherChina 2020 Review

titanxxh2020-12-012026-08-22

If you are a new gopher, I strongly recommend watching Go Programming Patterns. This conference featured many talks about frameworks. Of those, go-zero felt the most complete and the easiest for a small company or individual to adopt, and its author was passionate. I also recommend the talks on the Go compiler, the issues TiDB encountered, and Tantan. The weakest was a foreign speaker’s Go in the Cloud — Why People Choose Go for Cloud Computing…

The second day split into two tracks, so one person could hear only some of the talks. I bounced repeatedly between rooms 1 and 2 and selected a few. Unfortunately, I missed Alibaba’s EDAS talk, which was reportedly quite good.

The conference slides are available in this Git repository.

I unexpectedly ran into a former colleague at the venue—amazing. I was not certain at first because of the mask; only after sitting down and messaging on WeChat did we confirm it.

  1. Tantan ttdb
    Tantan, a co-organizer of the conference, is a social app for young people—though its promotional video at the venue seemed to include users in their sixties. Suppose you had to design a system that scanned the geographic paths of two people and matched those who had passed one another. How would you do it?
    They shared practical lessons from their own database:

    • SQL parsing optimizations such as predicate pushdown
    • Columnar storage, using cache coherence during the selection stage to improve performance
    • For failure detection, they chose centralized timeout detection instead of decentralized gossip. During recovery, rather than directing all traffic to a new replica and creating a spike, they occasionally send requests to replicas to keep data hot.
    • They use this library to detect goroutine leaks.

  2. Go Programming Patterns
    The talk covers small techniques we commonly use in ordinary code as well as ideas such as inversion of control. I especially recommend it to anyone new to Golang. Pay close attention to the idea of studying open-source code to learn good programming patterns. The author apparently knows the characteristics of many languages and therefore has plenty of complaints about Golang too.

    • Functional options: probably the most widely used pattern; virtually every major open-source project uses it. It elegantly distinguishes optional and required parameters, preserves internal encapsulation, makes calls self-documenting, and solves several related problems.
    • Go generation: because the language did not support generics—at least before 2021—using go generation well was often essential.
    • Error handling: the speaker mentioned an ancient Rob Pike article worth reading, though I am not sure which one. Go 1.13 added errors.Is and As to support error handling, while fmt.Errorf gained %w for wrapping errors.
    • For the Kubernetes visitor, decorators, pipelines, and so on, see the slides; the explanations there are clear.

  3. Grab Food
    Grab is another long-time GopherChina sponsor. It is a Southeast Asian behemoth that can be understood as Meituan + Alipay + Didi + Amap.
    The speakers first introduced Grab-Kit, their service framework. Like other frameworks, it supports middleware, validation, tracing, throttling, and so on, plus chaos for simulating failures—a chaos monkey?
    They then introduced Grab Food’s internal ML pipeline:

    1. Candidate set
    2. Filtering
    3. Reranking
    4. Post-ranking
      Citing a Google article, they listed current pain points in machine learning and explained how their Chimera system automates continuous integration, continuous training, and continuous delivery to achieve MLOps. They also mentioned using a multi-armed bandit A/B test to solve model-adaptation problems.

  4. go-chassis
    This was presented by someone from my company, so I will skip it.

  5. Functional Options and Config for APIs
    This talk overlapped with the second one above. Just watch that instead.

  6. Baidu BFE
    This speaker’s talk was not primarily technical, so I left after listening briefly. It discussed some aspects of technical management.

  7. go-zero
    Another framework talk, but this speaker was interesting. He said that although he was born in the 1970s, he still wrote code tirelessly. go-zero evolved through use inside his company, unlike some frameworks that may have been produced without a concrete business context. From the talk, go-zero’s functionality seemed very complete. It genuinely provides a one-stop solution to boilerplate development code. The speaker began by saying that he wanted his team’s style to be as consistent as possible, which was why the framework exists. Beyond go-zero itself, several solutions drawn from his product left a strong impression:

    • Program for failure.
    • Do not join!
    • Design caches correctly to prevent penetration, breakdown, and avalanche.
    • For load balancing, he mentioned the Power of Two Choices algorithm. It maximizes locality while dynamically avoiding excessive processing times on nearby services. These two articles—1 and 2—deserve careful study.
    • Adaptive circuit breaking: they abandoned Netflix’s Hystrix and introduced the Google SRE algorithm they use, including support for custom trigger conditions.
    • Adaptive load shedding.

  8. Go+
    Since its evangelist, Xu Shiwei, did not attend, a substitute presented at short notice and the result was poor. Despite piling up slides, the talk failed to explain why Go+ needed to replace Python for data science. Go+ can run by translating into Go and compiling, or by translating into bytecode and executing directly with somewhat lower performance. The latter feels pointless—why not use Python directly? It also did not support interfaces at the time and lacked runtime optimizations.

  9. PingCAP — Go Runtime-Related Problems in the TiDB Production Environment
    We had encountered the issues mentioned in this talk at work as well. The speaker is the author of Golang Internals.
    The first issue was higher latency caused by Golang’s goroutine scheduling. I do not really consider that a problem, or rather, that is simply how Golang’s goroutine scheduler is implemented. Unlike other languages, Golang supports coroutines natively and thereby commits to its implementation. Many other languages implement coroutines through libraries, allowing the scheduler implementation to be replaced according to need. Therefore, solving scheduling problems in Go may require pinning high-priority work to CPU cores.
    The third issue was a GC bug.
    The fourth concerned NUMA usage. The GC was not NUMA-aware and performed poorly on many-core systems after assist scanning was triggered during allocation. This has been optimized in current versions.

  10. Introduction to the Go Compiler — Shi Bin
    This speaker was truly hardcore: one of the fifty most prolific contributors to Golang, with commit access to Go’s Git repository and an annual invitation from the Go team to attend meetings in the United States.
    He explained that Go uses a self-hosted compiler rather than a general infrastructure such as LLVM. Many optimizations implemented in LLVM are therefore absent from the Golang compiler and must be added separately—perhaps that is where all your commits came from, master. There are 48 stages from Golang source to assembly; even three days would not have been enough for everything he could say. He ultimately selected several frontend, middle-end, and backend optimizations and explained his own patches, which was quite interesting.

  11. GORM Analysis and Best Practices
    This is probably the best Golang open-source project developed in China. Its author introduced the GORM 2.0 refactoring and design, along with some best practices. Interested readers can study it closely. I am not using it currently; once I do, I will return and review the talk.
