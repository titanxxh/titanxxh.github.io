Answering Zhihu: Are Board Games Doomed in China?

titanxxh2015-07-012026-08-22

This article originated as an answer on Zhihu.

The question was: Are board games in China destined to reach a dead end?
The argument was: compare the inconvenience of board games with the convenience of video games, and board games seem inherently disadvantaged as an organized activity.
The questioner appeared inclined to believe that board games in China would ultimately reach a dead end.

“I would like to hear veteran players’ views on the future of board games.” The questioner did not define a veteran, so as someone who has been in the hobby for six years, I will shamelessly offer an answer. I am purely a player of German-style strategy games, so my perspective on filler games and American-style games is inevitably incomplete. Please forgive that limitation.

My conclusion first: Board games in China are not destined to reach a dead end, but they are unlikely to become truly popular. My threshold for “popular” is actually low: the average person would need to take part in board-game activities more often each year than they go to the cinema, attend a live match, or engage in similar entertainment—assuming they are not already film or sports fans. At that point, the concept of board games would be widespread among the public and would have surpassed at least some other forms of entertainment.

The reason for this conclusion is simple. Zhang Jiawei once discussed “light appreciation” and “deep appreciation,” and I think that distinction is especially visible in board games. Board games tend to require deep appreciation, which makes it hard for them to become popular. Yet society will never lack the minority who seek that kind of experience, so the hobby will not reach a dead end.

The answer itself is now complete. Everything below is simply prompted by my own feelings.

The questioner compared board games and video games, so let me expand on that first. “Video games” is an enormous category encompassing mobile, PC, and console platforms and many genres. As a whole, video games are currently extremely popular. Divide them by genre, however, and you find that many have never been popular, while others enjoyed popularity for a long time and are now declining. Examples abound: the pinnacle of turn-based strategy, Heroes of Might and Magic III, was popular only briefly; the real-time strategy game Warcraft III remained popular for a long time but has declined in recent years. If the questioner still plays Dota, they should also know what a downward trend looks like. The decline of these games and the possible decline of board games share certain similarities. Why they declined has much to do with changes in the pace of people’s lives, but that is not the focus of this answer, so I will not elaborate.

Back to light and deep appreciation: why are board games hard to promote? Why are they niche entertainment both in China and abroad? In my view, it is closely connected to their tendency to demand deep appreciation. Players can certainly experience some of the fun and charm of board games very quickly through simple filler and party games. But that is only the tip of the iceberg, far removed from a complete understanding of the medium, so it counts as light appreciation. At the stage of light appreciation, people need little prior knowledge. You do not need deep artistic training or cultivation to decide whether a painting looks beautiful; if it feels right, you naturally respond. Deep appreciation, however, requires a reasonably thorough understanding of the thing itself. Consider watching football. If you not only find beauty in a goal or a skillful dribble, but also recognize the beauty of every brilliant save by the goalkeeper; a defensive shape maintained at all times; an accurate long pass from deep by a modern holding midfielder or defender with excellent vision; a manager’s emergency positional adjustment to restore control after losing the midfield; and home fans who never stop singing—then congratulations, you have entered the realm of deep appreciation. Without a substantial understanding of football, that is difficult to achieve.

Board games likewise support deep appreciation, but unlike football, they have few immediate thrills such as goals or dribbles that let outsiders discover their charm through light appreciation. In the mainstream board-game world—by which I mean German-style strategy games, and I ask fans of American-style games to go easy on me—it is difficult to provide sustained moments of excitement even for players, let alone spectators. Most of my board-game sessions take place in a store inside a shopping mall. The outer wall is glass, so people passing in the corridor can see us play, and some stop to watch. Ninety percent watch briefly and cannot understand what we are doing. The remaining ten percent enter and ask what we are playing; only five percent are willing to hear me explain what it is. How many ultimately fall down the rabbit hole, I do not know. This shows that board games struggle to attract someone at the level of light appreciation. The exact reasons are probably complex and lie outside my field, so I cannot explain them clearly.

At this point, many people may still wonder: after all this talk, what does deep appreciation of board games actually look like? To explain it well, I must use several specific games as examples. I prefer analyzing concrete cases because broad generalizations about board games are complicated. Players with little exposure to the hobby may find the examples hard to follow. If they interest you, find a local board-game group and experience these titles yourself.

(Beginning with simple board games.)

  1. Carcassonne

    Rules: players take turns drawing tiles and joining them together, scoring according to how the tiles connect—a scoring rule I will not spell out here. When all tiles have been used, the player with the most points wins.
    After hearing those rules as a spectator, can you tell what makes it fun? At most, the game’s light appeal may be: “Oh, the completed castle is enormous,” or “The artwork is attractive; that looks great.” After several games against an expert, however, you may begin to ask why you always lose. Why does every move your opponent makes score more on average? Why can your cities so often not be completed, halving their score? Why are all your meeples committed and impossible to recover? And so on. Watch several more games between experts and you may notice that their decisions differ greatly from yours, leading to a realization: every tile placement involves a great deal of judgment. Beyond maximizing your own score, you must limit your opponent’s score, and doing that well requires knowing how many copies of every tile remain.
    This example shows that deep appreciation of a board game may arise from refining one’s strategy toward perfection. Most German-style board games consist of decision after decision, so there are many similar examples; I chose the simplest.

  2. Chinatown

    Rules: the board contains dozens of numbered spaces. Players draw numbered shop-location cards and tiles representing different kinds of businesses. The game lasts several rounds. In each, players trade shop locations, shop tiles, and cash, then build shops together and calculate income. Connecting more shops of the same type increases their revenue. After the final round, the player with the most cash wins.
    What might light appreciation look like here? Assume the player has already been initiated through Carcassonne and knows to design a strategy around the rules. They realize that adjacent shops produce more income and work toward that goal, feeling pleased when they complete a large business. More often, however, random draws leave their shop locations and tiles poorly matched. When a trade is needed, the situation may not offer an obvious win-win exchange. The player cannot accurately estimate the value of costs and returns during negotiation, deals repeatedly collapse, and other players isolate them, producing a miserable score. After two more games, they may discover that negotiations work better if they do not state every benefit too bluntly or reveal too much of their position; that three-way trades can increase the chance of success; and that verbal promises can make future possibilities part of the transaction. And so on.
    This example shows that deep appreciation of a board game can come from moving beyond the literal rules and incorporating real-life experience and thought into play. Many role-playing games, cooperative games, and negotiation games have strong immersion. Players naturally begin talking, and with the right atmosphere, imaginative participants enrich the setting and collectively perform a vivid story.

  3. Twilight Struggle

    Rules: a world map contains many countries divided into regions. Two players represent the United States and Soviet Union through several decades of the Cold War—ten turns corresponding to its early, middle, and late periods. By playing cards, they increase their control of the world. Certain cards score according to control of a region. If one side’s score passes 20, that side wins. The score track is a tug of war, so points gained by your opponent subtract from yours. Otherwise, the higher score after ten turns wins.
    It must be noted that, at the time of writing, this is the highest-ranked game on BGG, the largest board-game website. I do not personally like it very much, but that does not diminish its greatness. Let me describe what I have seen many players experience. Beyond the thrill of controlling the whole picture as the leader of a superpower, every time you play a card, it describes an event, and the rulebook explains that event. You learn a piece of history. You are astonished, impressed, or amused; you look up captivating topics on Wikipedia in an attempt to reconstruct what happened; you sense the perfect harmony between the mechanics and history; you marvel at the labor the designers invested; you applaud and give the game your highest praise: a 10 on BGG. All of this depends on being willing to try the game more deeply—and meeting the right opponent.
    This example shows that deep appreciation of a board game can arise from curiosity about a buried period of history, a distant place, an ancient craft, or a departed soul. The player follows the game’s subject outward until it becomes a way of life. Many board-game settings draw on history, culture, art, and economics. Players encounter new knowledge that sparks curiosity and reflection.

Every board game has something distinctive and could support an essay of its own. I have used familiar classics here. If reading this makes people willing to learn more about board games and experience them, my goal is achieved. To return to the beginning, I do not expect board games to become a mass craze; I only hope more people will know them and that the hobby will become a little more widespread.
