[titanxxh's Essen 2015] 2. 7 Wonders: Duel Overview and Review

titanxxh2015-12-012026-08-23


First, some cosplay by overseas geeks to set the tone

After finishing the busy work on my thesis, I chose for the second installment of my Essen 2015 series a very popular game that I had missed during the previews: 7 Wonders: Duel, commonly called the two-player version of 7 Wonders. Overall, Duel makes numerous design improvements and provides an excellent play experience. Its three victory conditions are very well judged. Military and scientific victories are difficult but not impossibly distant, making this one of the finest two-player games.

“The beginning does not hint at the end.”
Herodotus

The designers placed this sentence from Herodotus, the “father of history,” at the very beginning of the rulebook. It seems both a verdict upon the ancient civilizations that created humanity’s greatest wonders and a message to the players: every game of 7 Wonders: Duel is full of uncertainty. I could not find the source of the quotation, however. Did the designers invent it?


Designers Bruno Cathala and Antoine Bauza

7 Wonders: Duel was designed by Antoine Bauza and Bruno Cathala. Bauza designed the original 7 Wonders and Tokaido, while Cathala created Abyss and Five Tribes. Both clearly have considerable experience with light strategy games. Their two-player collaboration worked extremely well, and I look forward to their future designs.

Game Overview

7 Wonders: Duel uses the setting and many mechanisms of the original 7 Wonders, but adapts them to the particular nature of two-player games, increasing competition and interaction.

Each player leads a civilization through three Ages, using cards to acquire coins, strengthen the military, develop science, and construct buildings and wonders.

During play, a player may win immediately through military or scientific supremacy. If nobody achieves either by the end of the third Age, the player with the highest total score wins.

Components

  • 1 game board
  • 23 Age I cards
  • 23 Age II cards
  • 20 Age III cards
  • 7 Guild cards
  • 12 Wonder cards
  • 4 Military tokens
  • 10 Progress tokens
  • 1 Conflict pawn, shown below after being painted by an overseas player
  • 31 coin tokens
    • 14 worth 1
    • 10 worth 3
    • 7 worth 6
  • 1 rulebook
  • 1 score pad
  • 1 player aid

Setup

  1. Place the game board to one side of the play area.
  2. Place the Conflict pawn in the center of the board.
  3. Place the four Military tokens in their corresponding spaces.
  4. Shuffle the ten Progress tokens, reveal five on the board, and return the rest to the box.
  5. Each player takes 7 coins.
  6. Players choose Wonders.
    1. Choose a starting player.
    2. Shuffle the twelve Wonder cards and reveal four at random. The starting player chooses one, the second player chooses two, and the starting player takes the remaining card.
    3. Reveal four more at random. The second player chooses one, the starting player chooses two, and the second player takes the last. Return the remaining four Wonder cards to the box.
  7. Build the three Age decks. Remove three cards face down from each Age. Choose three Guild cards face down and add them to the Age III deck. Shuffle all three decks separately.

Game Flow

The game lasts three Ages. It ends immediately if a player achieves scientific or military supremacy; otherwise, it ends after Age III.

At the beginning of each Age, arrange its twenty cards as illustrated for Age I, II, or III. The bottom row is always face up, and each higher row alternates between face up and face down.

Players alternate turns, with the starting player acting first in Age I. On a turn, choose one card that is not covered by any other card and perform one of three actions:

  • Construct a Building
    Pay the resources shown at the upper left of the chosen card, or use a prerequisite building’s chain symbol to build it for free. As in the original 7 Wonders, resources may come from your own city or be purchased from your opponent. The difference is that a resource costs 2 coins plus the number of matching resources on your opponent’s brown and gray cards, and the payment goes to the bank rather than the opponent.
  • Discard for Coins
    Discard the chosen card and gain 2 coins plus 1 for each yellow building in your city.
  • Construct a Wonder
    Pay the resources shown at the lower left of the Wonder card, then place the selected Age card face down beneath one of your unbuilt Wonders. Important: only seven Wonders may be constructed during the entire game. One player’s eighth Wonder will always remain unbuilt.

After a player uses a card, reveal any face-down card that is no longer covered.

When all twenty cards have been used, the Age ends. Set up the next Age, then the player with the weaker military position chooses its starting player. If the Conflict pawn stands exactly in the center, the player who took the final turn of the previous Age becomes the starting player.

Military

Every crossed-swords symbol on a building or Wonder moves the Conflict pawn one space toward the opponent. When it enters a zone containing a Military token, discard that token and force the opponent to lose the number of coins shown; if they have fewer, they lose all their coins. If the pawn reaches the final space, the opposing capital, that player immediately loses and you achieve military supremacy.

Science

The game contains seven kinds of scientific symbols. Whenever a player acquires a pair of identical symbols, they choose one Progress token from the board. A player who collects six different scientific symbols achieves scientific supremacy.

End of the Game

If neither player achieves military or scientific supremacy, the game ends after three Ages and scores are calculated.

  • Score points according to the position of the Conflict pawn.
  • Score blue, green, yellow, and purple cards.
  • Score Wonders.
  • Score Progress tokens.
  • Score 1 point for every 3 coins.

In a tie, the player with the most points on blue cards wins.

Review

7 Wonders: Duel solves many problems found in the original:

  1. Chain symbols for prerequisite buildings are now conspicuous at the top of each card rather than hidden beneath a pile, and later buildings show the same symbol within their costs.
  2. Interaction increases enormously. The criticism players most often make of the original 7 Wonders is insufficient interaction—really, the inability to interfere with distant players. Duel retains competition over military strength, resource purchasing, and scientific buildings, but adds even more important competition over card selection and turn order. Those two outstanding ideas lift the whole design to another level.
  3. It balances military, science, and points well, maintaining a delicate equilibrium between the three.

As for weaknesses, several Progress tokens feel rather useless. The Wonders are mostly well balanced, though one or two seem slightly weaker. I will not name names.

Overall, 7 Wonders: Duel genuinely offers everything a light strategy game should: attractive art; a perfect insert, even with sleeved cards; an appropriate sense of theme—the Conflict pawn makes a satisfying impact as it crashes across the board, and the limit of seven Wonders is clever, while civilization is honestly a difficult theme for a light game; and, most importantly, an experience that is simple yet deep. As mentioned earlier, players must consider not only what each card does but also the order in which cards are taken. Should you let your opponent reveal a face-down card? Can an extra-turn effect deny them a crucial building? You must also think about who begins each Age.

Some gameplay tips:

  • Production buildings—brown and gray—are important. The game strictly limits the supply of resources. If a player cannot produce a particular resource in Age I and fails to correct that in Age II, the rest of the game will be extremely difficult.
  • Commercial buildings—yellow—are important. First, players will often lack a resource. If the relevant production building was also removed from the game, a commercial building providing favorable trade for that missing resource becomes enormously useful later. Second, discarding cards for coins is common, and commercial buildings greatly increase the return from that action.
  • Wonders are important. In terms of raw card design, the standard return for a Wonder is 9 points, represented by the Pyramids of Giza. In actual games, few Wonders generate the equivalent of 9 points, so the Pyramids are a strong choice. Differing construction costs make direct comparisons difficult, however. Extra-turn effects on Wonders are excellent, but poor use can also backfire badly. The player who constructs fewer Wonders must find points elsewhere.
  • Watch science and military strength. Do not let your opponent use an extra turn to reveal a face-down card and then win immediately through scientific or military supremacy.
  • Choosing the starting player is crucial, especially after both players have exhausted their extra-turn effects.
