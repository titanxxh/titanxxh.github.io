[Board Game Life] Issue 5: On Strategic Depth in Board Games

titanxxh2015-06-302026-08-22

This article first appeared on Baidu’s Board Games forum. The circumstances behind it are complicated, so I will omit them here.

When it comes to strategic depth, I have not played as many board games as the experts on the forum, nor can I claim to have mastered more than a handful. Even so, I believe I am capable of drawing a few conclusions.
This expands on a reply I posted yesterday.

Can a game’s strategic depth be quantified? Personally, I believe it can, but the problem is complicated enough that even a full paper would not be sufficient to explain it in detail.
First, I must point out that directly comparing strategic depth across different types of games is difficult, though indirect comparison is possible. To keep this article from becoming so long that it loses focus, everything below concerns comparisons between games of the same type.

A game’s strategic richness can be divided qualitatively into breadth and depth.

Let us begin with breadth. Put simply, it is the number of meaningful decisions available to a player each turn in the current state—considering their own player board or hand and, when information is public, the other players’ situations. Because these are board games, designers generally do not approach them as they would video games, so a player rarely has many meaningful options at a decision point, usually somewhere from zero to ten. Games with higher weight ratings may increase that number to some extent. The important point is that breadth is driven primarily by the options the designer provides; the player then combines those options with the rules to make a decision. Consider Agricola. Its worker-placement spaces are the ones the designer created, with new ones added each round. To take an action, players must pay certain resources or satisfy certain conditions. How might we increase the game’s strategic breadth? Easy: add more action spaces, introduce more types of resources—as in Ora et Labora—or impose additional conditions. But there is a limit to useful breadth. As noted above, designing a board game is not the same as designing a video game; endlessly adding options or restrictions is not necessarily beneficial. Designers’ notes often reveal that early prototypes contained very concrete, abundant detail, which designers gradually refined as development proceeded.
In summary, breadth as one aspect of strategy depends largely on the designer. The player’s own thinking accounts for a relatively small part here: they merely check whether their current state satisfies certain conditions, and with computer assistance even that step could be skipped.

Depth, on the other hand, is what I believe has the greater influence on a board game’s strategic richness. Depth is less intuitive to define than breadth. Broadly, it determines how many layers a player must consider when making a decision. “Layers” has a rich meaning here and generally includes, but is not limited to, the following aspects:

  • A player’s own medium- and long-term planning

This is clearly reflected in most games. Players need to establish a medium- or long-term plan; many games, for example Tzolk’in, require you to build a point-scoring engine. In strategically rich games, a player with a plan will generally gain an advantage over one who treats every turn in isolation, or will at least think more efficiently. If it is more difficult for a player to establish a clear plan in one game than in another, we can say the former has greater strategic depth.

  • How to make trade-offs when conflicts arise

Many games feature competition for resources, one of the relatively few forms of player interaction in Eurogames. This easily creates situations where you cannot have your cake and eat it too. Conflicts over resources then affect short-term and even long-term plans. “Resources” here means anything concrete or abstract that helps a player win: wood or stone in Agricola; the choice between settlements in Ora et Labora—different settlements can shift the entire focus of your later strategy; or even the alliances you forge and enemies you make in negotiation games. The more conflicts a game asks players to balance, and the harder it is to determine the basis for those trade-offs, the greater its relative strategic depth.

  • How to control randomness

Games with shared decks, bags of tiles, dice, and similar elements introduce randomness, but the amount of randomness does not directly determine strategic depth. A well-designed game can include substantial randomness while giving players mechanisms to control it; The Castles of Burgundy and Troyes are classic examples. A poorly designed game instead leaves players feeling helpless before fate. I will avoid naming examples for fear of provoking outrage. If a game’s mechanisms keep random events from causing wild swings in a player’s position, we can say that game has greater strategic depth.

  • Identifying your principal opponent, especially with hidden information

This issue generally arises when victory information or other key information is hidden or only partly public, forcing players to identify their opponents. Even in games with fully public information, however, identifying the right opponent is not necessarily simple. A few examples make both points clear. Legends of the Three Kingdoms contains hidden identities, yet identifying opponents is fairly easy: after one round, most roles are clear and do not change as play continues. In Modern Art, the key resource—money—and the composition of other players’ hands are hidden, making the principal opponent difficult to identify and causing that opponent to change continually. Then there is Ora et Labora, a zero-randomness game with completely public information. Even after more than forty plays, I still find it hard before Phase D to judge whether players of comparable skill are slightly ahead of me or slightly behind. The harder it is to identify one’s opponent and the more frequently that opponent changes, the greater a game’s relative strategic depth.

  • How to constrain your opponent

In traditionally American-style games, methods of constraining opponents are often direct. In traditional Eurogames, they tend to be less direct, and their design generally discourages players from focusing so much on interference that they neglect their own development. This point is closely related to the one above. If you identify the wrong opponent, or fail to notice that your principal rival has changed, you may target the wrong person with the wrong disruption. That not only harms their experience but can, more seriously, unbalance the entire game. In general, the less obvious a game’s methods of constraining opponents, the higher their cost, and the harder it is to identify the correct opponent, the greater the game’s strategic depth.

  • How to avoid being constrained, and how to keep your second-best options from becoming too poor when you are constrained—in other words, raising your floor

This last item is the only one listed here that requires interpreting another person’s thoughts. As the saying goes, you may know a person’s face but not their heart; human intentions are hard to fathom. It also interacts with the previous point: how you think about constraining an opponent largely determines how that opponent thinks about constraining you. The topic is too complex for me to explain clearly because it involves judging your opponent’s level of reasoning. But I do have experience with the latter question: keeping second-best options acceptable after being blocked. As an undergraduate, I had a close friend who played often. In pure tactical calculation, I admit he was better than me, though he also fell into analysis paralysis far more often. But he tended to focus excessively on his own development and overlook how others might affect him. This was less obvious in two-player games and especially pronounced in multiplayer ones. Sometimes his move depended entirely on whether the other two or three players blocked his intended action. If anyone did, his entire plan collapsed; if nobody did, he executed an optimal decision smoothly. Remember that you are playing with people. You cannot fully understand every player’s reasoning or model everyone as a perfectly rational decision-maker. Therefore, when making decisions, it is necessary not only to maximize the expected value of your future positions but also to reduce their variance. I will refrain from judging which games are strategically deeper on this final point…

The discussion above shows how difficult it is to analyze strategic depth, even qualitatively. Only after mastering a game—which usually requires a major investment of time—can one confidently attempt a full quantitative analysis.
