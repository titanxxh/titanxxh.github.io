[titanxxh's Essen 2014] Game Overview 6 and Unboxing: Fields of Arle

titanxxh2015-06-302026-08-23


Fields of Arle

This Essen 2014 installment features another new money pit from Uwe Rosenberg: Fields of Arle, sometimes described as a two-player Agricola on a grand scale. Arle is a small village in East Frisia, northern Germany—a place almost nobody outside the area would know. The game is somewhat autobiographical: Arle is where Uwe’s father was born, while Uwe himself was born twenty kilometers away in Aurich, which also appears in the game.
The setting returns us to the Middle Ages and the glory days of the village of Arle. Though Arle was not large, its high-quality flax contributed to Frisia’s prosperity. Players begin from nothing in a “one-horse town” with only one flax field—and literally one horse in the setup—then gradually develop it into a major textile town.


Front of the box

Introduction

Welcome to East Frisia!

Arle is a small village in East Frisia. Today, only the scale of its cathedral reminds people of its former importance.
In centuries past, Arle stood out because of the fertile farmland around it. The finest flax in northern Germany was said to grow there. As demand for the resource rose, local farmers quickly became wealthy and lived better than other East Frisians. Growing prosperity also gave people freedom to decide whether to cultivate flax personally or pursue other needs.

You may grow wheat, raise animals, harvest peat, colonize moorland, or build dikes. You may cultivate forests, travel by vehicle, or construct workshops and inns. Your most important task is to build the renowned cathedral mentioned above.
The game consists of nine “half years,” alternating between summer and winter. Different actions are available in the two seasons. A season-specific inventory occurs after each half year, and final scoring determines who achieved more over four and a half years.

For those who come from the sea,
East Frisia is not at the end of the world,
but rather at its very beginning.

Ewald Christophers

Components

  • One large main game board
  • 2 home boards
  • 2 “travel destination/barn” storage boards
  • 1 supply board
  • 9 travel destinations per player
  • Building materials:
    • 24 clay/bricks
    • 24 wood/timber
  • Clothing:
    • 24 linen/summer wear
    • 24 leather/leather wear
    • 24 woolen cloth/winter wear
  • Replacement pieces:
    • 1 5× linen
    • 1 5× woolen cloth
    • 1 5× leather
  • 1 scoring pad
  • 2 overview sheets
  • 1 sticker sheet
  • Tiles:
    • 5 forest/park
    • 7 stall/depot
    • 3 stable/double stall
    • 14 wheat field/flax field
    • 3 cart/horse cart
    • 3 carriage/droshky
    • 2 handcart/wagon
    • 6 peat boat/plow
    • 10 dikes
    • 2 paths
    • 6 moor/drained moor
    • 31 buildings
  • Wooden indicators:
    • 10 tool indicators per player
    • 1 wheat indicator per player
    • 1 flax indicator per player
    • 1 hide indicator per player
    • 1 wool indicator per player
    • 2 food indicators per player
  • Wooden tokens:
    • 4 workers per player
    • 40 peat
    • 20 sheep
    • 20 cattle
    • 20 horses
  • 1 half-year marker
  • 1 starting-player marker

Setup

  1. Put the supply board between the players and arrange the supply piles as shown. If the table is not large enough, simply leave this board in the box.

    For pieces in the top row, a player may freely choose either side when receiving one during the game.
    Pieces in the bottom row may be used only on their front side; the reverse is the upgraded version.

  2. Place the large game board next to the supply board. Put the orange half-year marker on space one of the round track. Each player chooses a color and places their tool indicators on the leftmost spaces of the ten work tracks.

    Tool indicators show how many tools you own, and that quantity can increase during play.

  3. Familiarize yourself with building costs, which are printed on the board.

  4. Choose the starting player randomly and give them the starting-player marker. Put one worker from each player on every space from July through October, with the starting player’s worker on top.

  5. Place buildings according to their colors on the board. Some colors have more buildings than spaces; remove the extras from the game.

    There are two sets of green buildings. For a first game, use the four light-green buildings. Once familiar with the game, use four of the seven dark-green buildings instead.

  6. Take a home board and arrange every component on it as shown.

    The travel-experience track is on the left, empty land in the center, and goods tracks on the right.
    In addition to the home board, take a “travel destination/barn” storage board and the set of travel destinations in your color. Place them on the storage board. The barn is beneath the destinations and begins empty.
    You receive:

    • One overview sheet
    • 4 wood, 4 clay, and 3 peat

    The result should look roughly like this:

Because Uwe’s rules are so verbose, I improvised the material that follows. This installment is therefore not really a rules translation; at most, it is a detailed introduction.

The objective is to develop your village as much as possible over nine rounds. The player with the highest final score wins. Numbers inside compass roses indicate points. The main ways to score are:

  • Advance your dikes. As they move forward, they unlock more land; in the final row, they also prevent certain penalties.
  • Points from buildings.
  • Points from stables and stalls, as well as from animals.
  • Travel experience.
  • Draining moors reduces penalties, and harvesting all their peat reduces them further.
  • Cloth and clothing.
  • Forests and parks.
  • Goods, whose points can be doubled with a depot.
  • Plows, peat boats, and vehicles.

Gameplay

The game lasts nine rounds. Each represents half a year, alternating between winter and summer, and the game ends after the fifth summer.
Every round has three phases.

Preparation Phase

Players put their four workers on the corresponding months—July through October in summer, January through April in winter—with the starting player’s workers on top.

Work Phase

From left to right, players perform actions one at a time, with the starting player acting first.
Summer actions are available only in summer rounds and winter actions only in winter rounds. Each season has fifteen different actions. They are all printed on the board, so I will not translate them here. Content inside the blue-and-white Frisian pattern represents a cost; actions generally require payment.

Only one player may perform one special action—an action from the other season—in a round. Their opponent receives the starting-player marker afterward. If nobody uses a special action, the starting-player marker still changes hands.

At any time, a player may:

  • Load goods onto vehicles. Loading flips an item to its other side. If it is a travel destination, goods may be sold for food. One large vehicle space cannot hold two small goods, but two small spaces can hold one large good.
  • Rearrange animals.
  • If they own a peat boat, exchange goods at a 1:1 rate.

Inventory Phase

After both players complete the Work phase, conduct the season’s inventory.

Summer inventory:

  1. Unload vehicles. Players receive the goods or travel experience they contain.
  2. Milk animals. Owning 2/5/7 sheep or 1/3/5 cattle yields 1/2/3 food.
  3. Harvest. Every wheat field on a home board produces one wheat, every flax field one flax, and every forest one wood.
  4. Subsistence. Pay three food and two peat—winter is coming!

Winter inventory:

  1. Unload vehicles as above.
  2. Breed animals. Only animals in stalls and stables reproduce, and there must be room for the offspring, since young animals live with their parents. Every pair of the same animal produces one new animal.
  3. Shear sheep. Owning 1/4/6 sheep yields 1/2/3 wool.
  4. Subsistence. Pay three food.

Game End

After nine rounds, the game ends. Players may use their peat boats to exchange goods, then score:

  • Clothing and building materials, with halves rounded down.
  • Items in the barn.
  • Travel experience.
  • Tool levels on the main board.
  • Each good scores according to its quantity on the goods track. If the player owns a depot, double the points in this category.
  • Points and penalties on the home board.
  • Animal points equal twice the number of the least numerous animal plus the number of the second-least numerous animal.
  • Failing to meet subsistence during an Inventory phase causes penalties here, although anyone who has played Uwe’s other games is unlikely to let that happen.

Solo Variant

The player cannot use special actions. A score of 110 is considered highly commendable!
