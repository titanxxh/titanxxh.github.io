[titanxxh's Essen 2015] 1. Among Nobles Overview, Unboxing, and Review

titanxxh2015-11-092026-08-23

Xiaoda’s new Essen games finally arrived. In the middle of the week, E—who actually ambushed Xiaoda at home, absolutely deranged—I, and Bowl King could not wait any longer and went to Club A to play several new titles. The first installment of Essen 2015 will take a detailed look at what has been called the year’s most underrated game: Among Nobles.


Among Nobles

Overview

Among Nobles is a card game in which each player leads a family in feudal Europe. You expand your family and use marriage, war, and intrigue to pursue victory. Players control some of the most influential and iconic figures in European history, including Napoleon, Charles I, and Catherine the Great, activating sequences of abilities on their cards. When a female character marries, she joins her husband’s family, after which the couple can be activated together. The right—or wrong—marriage can decide the game. The player with the most Prestige Points at the end wins.

Components

  • 1 rulebook
  • 60 character cards
    • 6 starting Lady cards
    • 6 starting Lord cards
    • 16 Era I characters
    • 16 Era II characters
    • 16 Era III characters
  • 9 Province cards
  • 1 Starting Player card
  • 45 coin tokens
  • Prestige Point tokens
    • 50 one-point Prestige tokens
    • 50 five-point Prestige tokens
  • Player tokens in five colors—black, white, orange, purple, and gray. Color-blind friendly; excellent!
    • 3 Action tokens (discs)
    • 10 Army tokens (cubes)

Character Cards

Male characters have brown backgrounds and female characters light backgrounds. The colors of the bars above and below each card indicate the character’s traits; a couple’s traits determine those of their children.

  • Red: Commander, skilled at military affairs and actions in the first row.
  • Blue: Devout, skilled at piety and actions in the second row.
  • Yellow: Merchant, skilled at commerce and actions in the third row.
  • Green: Negotiator, skilled at intrigue and actions in the fourth row.

A character’s abilities appear beneath the portrait.

  • (Crown) Noble: the ability applies to this character permanently.
  • (Stork carrying a bundle) Baptism: the ability triggers when this character is born.
  • (Church) Alliance: the ability triggers when this character marries.
  • (Grim Reaper) Legacy: the ability triggers when this character dies.
  • (Scroll) Intrigue: a special ability triggered when the character takes an Intrigue action.

Setup

  1. Lay out the nine Province cards in a 3 × 3 grid. In a three-player game, remove Holland, Normandy, and Vienna—marked 4+ in the lower-right—and arrange the remaining Provinces in a 2 × 3 grid.
  2. In a three-player game, remove all green cards.
  3. Shuffle each era’s character cards separately.
  4. Reveal seven Era I characters, or five in a three-player game. These represent children who may be born in the future.
  5. Place the Prestige Points and coins nearby.
  6. Each player chooses a color and takes its Action tokens—use only two per player in a five-player game—and Army tokens. Not all ten Army tokens are available at the beginning; additional tokens must first enter your personal supply through certain effects.
  7. Each player takes a reference card.
  8. Each player receives two Armies and one coin.
  9. Determine the starting player: all players simultaneously reveal one to five fingers. The player showing the greatest unique number becomes starting player; if the highest numbers tie, use the next lower unique number. For example, with 5, 5, 4, 4, and 3, the player showing 3 starts.
  10. Distribute the starting families. First separate the six starting couples into three groups, A, B, and C, according to their card backs:
  • Three players: because green cards are unused, each group contains only one couple. Player 3 chooses a couple, Player 1 chooses one, and Player 2 receives the remaining pair.
  • Four players: Player 4 chooses a couple and gives the other pair in that group to Player 2. Player 1 chooses a couple and gives the other pair in that group to Player 3.
  • Five players: Player 5 chooses a couple and gives the other pair in that group to Player 3. Player 1 chooses a couple and gives the other pair in that group to Player 4. Player 2 chooses either couple from the remaining group.
  1. Each player places their Lady card underneath their Lord card so that only her action area remains visible.

Tips

Coins matter! Sons matter! Marriage matters! Remember these facts before your first game, or your actions will become very awkward.

Game Flow

The game lasts three eras.

Each era contains several rounds. Players first take actions during every round, followed by an Upkeep Phase. When the final character of the current era appears, a generational change occurs during the next round’s Upkeep Phase. The game ends after the third generational change.

Action Phase

Beginning with the starting player, players take turns performing one action and placing an Action token on the character card being activated, continuing until every Action token has been used. Note: in the first round of the game, each player has only one action.

  • Activate: activate an unmarried man, unmarried woman, or couple that has not yet been activated. Choose one of the four action rows and resolve its icons strictly from left to right.
  • Reactivate: after every unmarried man and couple in your family has been activated, you may reactivate an already activated character. You may resolve only one action icon from the first column of one row. Note: if a character lacks an icon in the first column of a row, that row cannot be used for reactivation.

Action icons:

  • (Crossed swords) Military: choose one of the following.
    • Pay one coin to move one Army from your personal supply to any Province.
    • Move one Army from one Province to any other Province for free; the Provinces need not be adjacent.
  • (Cross) Piety: gain 1 Prestige Point.
  • (Coin) Commerce: gain one coin.
  • (Gender symbol) Procreation: choose a character from the display to become this couple’s child. A boy costs one coin. Place the newborn character one row beneath the couple to show that it belongs to the next generation. Note: this action is mandatory unless you cannot pay its cost, the display contains no child with suitable traits, or the icon was triggered by one of your third-generation couples. If the newborn character has a shield icon in the upper-right, add one Army token to your personal supply.
  • (Pair of rings) Marriage: pay two coins for a wedding. Choose an unmarried man in your family as the groom. Every other player must offer an unmarried woman. Return the unchosen women; the selected bride’s family gains 2 Prestige Points. Move the bride’s card into the groom’s family and place it beneath him to form a couple.
  • (Scroll) Intrigue: resolve the character’s Intrigue ability. For a couple, use the husband’s ability. The icon has no effect on a character without an Intrigue ability.
  • (Trading hands) Hire: pay two coins, then take either a Military, Piety, or Marriage action.

Upkeep Phase

  1. Determine control of the Provinces. Resolve each Province in turn. If it contains Army tokens of more than one color, every player present removes one Army at a time until at most one player has any Army there. That player controls the Province.
  2. Gain Province income. Each Province’s controller receives the Prestige Points and/or coins it provides.
  3. Reset characters by removing Action tokens from character cards.
  4. If no displayed character was taken, discard all of them.
  5. Generational change: if one was triggered in the previous round’s Upkeep Phase, the oldest generation of every player’s family—the top row—dies, triggering any Legacy abilities. If a player has no male heir, reveal cards from the top of the deck until a male appears. He automatically becomes that player’s male heir and marries an unmarried woman of the oldest generation, if available. Shuffle all other revealed characters back into the deck.
  6. Refill the character display to its starting size. If the final character of the current era is revealed, a generational change will occur during the next Upkeep Phase.
  7. Pass the Starting Player card clockwise.

End of the Game

After the third generational change, the game ends. Players may exchange every three coins for 1 Prestige Point. The player with the most Prestige wins. If tied, the player controlling the most Provinces wins; if still tied, play again!

Now let us examine several characters from the game:

  • Felipe II de España — Philip II of Spain

    Philip II was Habsburg king of Spain from 1556 to 1598 and king of Portugal—as Philip I—from 1581. His reign marked the height of Spanish power. Historians often describe this period as the era of Habsburg domination of Europe. Ambitious, Philip tried to maintain a vast Catholic empire but ultimately failed.

    But why is his Intrigue ability designed to remove an unmarried woman in exchange for two coins and two points?

  • Henry VIII of England

    Henry VIII (June 28, 1491–January 28, 1547), the second son of Henry VII, was the second Tudor king and succeeded to the throne on April 22, 1509. He was also Lord of Ireland and later King of Ireland. Seeking to set aside his wife and marry a new queen, Henry quarreled with the pope and launched the English Reformation. Important acts allowed him to remarry, made the English bishops leaders of the Church of England, separated the English church from Rome, and established Henry as England’s supreme religious authority. He dissolved the monasteries, bringing royal power to its peak, and incorporated Wales into England. Although some say Henry became an Anglican before his death, he promoted Catholic rites and doctrines throughout his life. His descendants Edward VI and Elizabeth I continued the Reformation, while his daughter Mary I restored Catholicism during her reign. Henry VIII married six times and ordered two of his wives beheaded.
    Beyond the Reformation, he actively encouraged humanist scholarship. His union of England and Wales brought royal power to its height. State power expanded, as did middle-class participation in politics. Henry successfully involved England in continental diplomacy, but exhausted the treasury in doing so and left problems for later monarchs.

    His Intrigue ability is entirely fitting: remove his own wife to gain three coins and three points.

  • Henriette Marie de France — Henrietta Maria of France

    Henrietta Maria was a French princess, daughter of Henry IV and Queen Marie de’ Medici, sister of Louis XIII, and aunt of Louis XIV. She was born at the Louvre. Her father was assassinated before she turned one, and her mother was banished from court in 1617.
    Considered as a bride for the English crown prince in the 1620s, she married Charles I—already king—at St Augustine’s Church in Canterbury on June 13, 1625, becoming queen of England, Scotland, and Ireland. Their marriage was initially far from harmonious. As a Catholic, she brought many Catholic servants and clergy from France and practiced Catholic rites openly, provoking distrust throughout England and displeasing the king, who eventually forced her to send most of her French attendants home. The king was also exceptionally close to the Duke of Buckingham—rumors described their relationship as homosexual—leading to continual marital disputes.

    Is that why her card can produce children without a man?

  • Napoléon Bonaparte

    Does he really need an introduction?

    His abilities are nothing but swords—and every time he moves an Army into a Province containing someone else’s Army, he gains a point! What an ability! Worthy of one of Europe’s four greatest commanders!

First-Play Session Report

First, the game’s best player count should be four. Unfortunately, my two experiences were a three-player game and watching a five-player game. I still need to arrange a four-player session later.

My first game was with E and Bowl King. Two rounds after the rules explanation and start of play, E flipped the table because his colors could not produce a son, blocking his actions. But E had chosen a starting couple first, and I had emphasized the importance of sons beforehand, so he has only himself to blame…

After restarting, I was second in turn order and had to take the remaining couple. As a result, one of my actions was blocked during both the second and third rounds. Bowl King controlled two Provinces early while marrying off a daughter, creating an enormous scoring lead. E and I earned relatively little by marrying off daughters, and with no meaningful source of income, I headed straight for last place.

Since the game includes opportunities to attack one another, the leader naturally becomes a target. In the middle game, E married one of my daughters, instantly reviving my economy. As everyone accumulated more couples, action quality became critical. Bowl King foolishly married one of my useless daughters simply because she was pretty, and gradually lost his advantage.

Late in the game, several Armies produced earlier by Charles I—who adds an Army whenever a son is born—let me control more Provinces. I also assembled one couple that could score four points per round, raising my scoring efficiency, plus a character who generated three coins each round to keep the economy running. Even when E repeatedly used Intrigue to smash several of my Armies at the end, I retained control of two Provinces. The first game ended with me on 53, Bowl King on 50, and E on 46.

First Impressions

Whether judged by its mechanisms or card design, the game integrates its setting very well. Players constantly discover thematic connections during play, which is delightful. The rules are not complicated. The main questions are which player to target, which children to choose, how to arrange marriages, and what your scoring path will be. Individual actions are generally easy to decide, giving the game medium strategic depth but high interaction. The character portraits are also attractive.

Three-player games omit some characters, while five-player games give each player only two actions and feel awkward. Poor flexibility across player counts is one of the game’s major flaws.

Some lessons from play:

  • Coins are important! To maximize action efficiency, you need marriages, and marriages cost money. Early in the game, you also need Armies to secure the income from one or two Provinces, and placing Armies costs money too.
  • Too many Armies are useless. In the middle and late game they enter and leave the board frequently; roughly five is enough.
  • Producing too many children is equally useless. Focus on the quality of the couples in your family and improve the value of each action.

Among Nobles suits players who enjoy historical settings—especially European history—or who enjoy immersive Eurogames and do not mind a little direct aggression.
