[A Life in Board Games] Episode 6: A Brief Look at Uwe Rosenberg's Design Philosophy

titanxxh2015-11-012026-08-22

This article first appeared last year on Yikeguan’s WeChat public account and was also posted on the Board Games Tieba forum. I had barely played Caverna or Fields of Arle then, so I did not write much about them. This is a good opportunity to update it.

Uwe Rosenberg!

Uwe Rosenberg is rendered on Chinese BGG as Wu Rose Mountain, but Chinese players more affectionately call him “Teacher Uwe.” Far too many introductions to Uwe already exist online, so I will not repeat them; interested players can search a wiki. Whenever his designs are discussed, opinions vary widely. Those who dislike them can usually produce a long list of reasons, and “recycling old ideas” would probably appear on every list. Those who love Uwe—especially devoted fans like me—always recognize something beloved in each new game and become excited by every surprise created through a change in his design. Whatever individual players think of him, Uwe’s status in board gaming is beyond dispute. Consider the BGG rankings: Agricola was 6th, Le Havre 15th, At the Gates of Loyang 190th, Ora et Labora 47th, Glass Road 176th, Caverna: The Cave Farmers 3rd, and Fields of Arle 106th. Even the two-player versions of Agricola and Le Havre achieved respectable rankings, at 108 and 604. Rankings do not represent everything, but so many highly placed works at least demonstrate an outstanding design philosophy maintained at an exceptionally high level over many years. When thinking about that philosophy, I have sometimes wondered whether Uwe studied the Chinese Book of Changes. Something always changes within what remains constant, while something constant is always concealed within change. The proportion is consistently just right, and many of those changes genuinely improve the games enormously.

Across Uwe’s designs—from Agricola, Le Havre, and At the Gates of Loyang, jokingly called the “hunger trilogy,” to Ora et Labora, Glass Road, and Caverna—every title bears his distinctive style regardless of its specific mechanisms. They support solo play, provide a good experience at different player counts, offer several strategic paths, and use related economic themes. Seven years passed, yet Uwe’s central philosophy remained unchanged. At the level of detail, however, each title introduced many admirable changes. Let me select several games I know relatively well and analyze them.

Agricola

Agricola was not Uwe’s first design, but many players probably discovered him through it. As his breakthrough work, its arrival in distant 2007 ended Puerto Rico’s long reign, removing that game from first place and elevating itself to the pantheon. Even today, any veteran discussing Agricola will almost certainly praise it highly. Agricola is a classic worker-placement game, but it incorporates several then-novel details that greatly enrich play: available action spaces increase as the game progresses; occupation and minor-improvement cards are unique to each player; resources accumulate from round to round; and so forth. These details are also interconnected, so the design feels cohesive rather than patched together. Actions introduced later in the game should naturally be more attractive, for example, but allowing resources to accumulate ensures that early spaces do not become useless. Occupations and minor improvements give players individual approaches, preventing competition for shared resources from becoming too fierce. Most praiseworthy of all is resource accumulation. This greatly simplifies balance because it is self-balancing: when the resources on an action space are insufficiently attractive, nobody chooses it, and the amount eventually grows until it becomes worthwhile. Conversely, a player may take a space even before its resources meet their own expectations, anticipating that another round of growth would make it irresistible to somebody else. You can rarely wait for the perfect moment to collect exactly the right resources. The whole game therefore contains a beauty of imperfection. Is life not the same? Yet what makes a design can also undo it. Occupations and minor improvements enormously increase replayability, but excessively powerful cards and combinations inevitably damage balance. Concentrating many decisions at the beginning of the game, as Seasons also does, has caused considerable controversy and is unfriendly to newcomers. Feeding feels slightly too tense and its penalties too severe, leaving players frustrated. Resource accumulation is excellent, but replenishing every space becomes tedious late in the game. Many groups have surely tried to speed things up by having several players replenish resources together, only to add something twice or omit it entirely. Overall, Agricola remains an unsurpassed masterpiece for many players, the foundation of Uwe’s whole body of work, and a source from which countless later designers have borrowed.

Le Havre

In 2008, Uwe went a step further with the second title in his harvest trilogy, Le Havre. Agricola and Le Havre once stood together in BGG’s top five, a glorious achievement that Uwe’s fans loved to mention. Agricola’s influence is obvious in Le Havre, but so are many noteworthy changes. The number of workers falls to one; players can borrow their primary resource, money, from the bank; buildings enter play randomly within certain limits; and more. Uwe seems to have recognized several obvious shortcomings in Agricola and optimized them. Feeding is no longer as oppressive, since in the worst case a player can take loans. The most conspicuous change is resource replenishment. Rather than adding large quantities of resources at the start of a round, two fixed resources are added at the beginning of each player’s turn. This avoids the duplicates and omissions of Agricola. Replenishment also becomes a metronome for the game. Because this metronome has seven beats, every player follows the same feeding rhythm but receives a different number of actions, giving each a distinct tempo. This design helps stagger the players’ strategic plans. A fixed replenishment rhythm has its own limitations, such as making it difficult to introduce new resource types and keeping accumulation speeds constant. For those reasons, some players rank Le Havre above Agricola, while others consider it far inferior.

Ora et Labora

In 2009, Uwe concluded the harvest trilogy with the somewhat lighter At the Gates of Loyang, which I will not discuss in detail. After two quiet years, he appeared at the fair with a new design: my personal favorite, Ora et Labora, which my friends and I simply call Ora. If I had to summarize its design in one word, that word would be elegance. When teaching the game, players unfamiliar with Uwe may see its small rondel and feel curiosity or even confusion. Veterans of his work, however, will surely applaud the brilliance of the design. A circle truly is a perfect shape. People learn to handle life smoothly after being polished by experience, and this rondel seems similarly refined by Uwe’s years of work, eventually becoming such an elegant solution: move the pointer gently, and everything is done. Tzolk’in later borrowed something of this idea. The mechanism solves Agricola’s tedious replenishment, incorporates Le Havre’s metronome, and makes introducing new resources simple. The rondel does have limitations. Agricola can give resources of the same type different growth rates, for example, whereas this system cannot. That does not prevent us from praising its elegance. Ora also introduces the relative positions of buildings, substantially changes scoring, and raises the level of thought required. Players must decide not only when to buy which building but also where to place it, maximizing the score of their settlements. This demands stronger overall strategy. Perhaps because of the added complexity, Uwe removed every random element and fixed the exact quantity of all buildings, reducing the cognitive burden. The familiar feeding phase from earlier designs also disappears, replaced by a subtler and more flexible settlement-building phase. Players no longer become frustrated by insufficient food, and they have choices when building settlements. If a high-scoring settlement is temporarily unaffordable, they can build one worth slightly less, conserve resources, and prepare earlier for the later game. Since a settlement scores mainly through surrounding buildings, its own value does not affect the final total too severely, making the system friendlier. Another strength is player interaction. When another player constructs a building before you, that is not necessarily bad: you may still use it, perhaps even gaining its benefit before its owner, while saving the resources required to build it. On the other hand, using it lets your opponent cycle their worker sooner while slowing your own worker rotation. Finally, Ora’s finest quality, in my opinion, is its kindness to trailing players. In many games, someone whose early development goes wrong—because a building is taken or a route disrupted—has effectively left the competition. Here, until the final phase, that player still has a chance to allocate resources intelligently, convert them into points, and catch up. For all these reasons, Ora et Labora became my favorite game and the one I have played most, without exception. It is also one of the few games I rated a perfect ten on BGG. A high rating may result from original design, polished mechanisms, or an excellent play experience. A perfect score, I think, has only one cause: the player’s deep love. If loving the right person at the right time is called happiness, then meeting the right people and playing the right game at the right time must be another kind of happiness.

Glass Road

The following year, Uwe released two-player versions of Agricola and Le Havre. They streamlined the originals without losing their strategy and make good choices for a casual game with your partner. In 2013, Uwe released two titles at once, Glass Road and Caverna. Because Glass Road was shorter, my friends and I played it more often. Uwe adopted a simplified form of Ora et Labora’s building system, dividing buildings into three types: conversion buildings, one-use buildings, and scoring buildings. He also retained Ora’s concept of resource conversion to drive progress. The principal innovation is that players no longer stack resources in front of themselves. Instead, a rondel similar to Ora’s records each quantity. The deeper reason for this design is that obtaining an advanced resource consumes one of each basic resource, precisely represented by moving the pointer one space. Is that not elegant? The rules for playing character cards are equally clever. Each round, a player chooses five of fifteen characters but may actively play only three. The other two must be triggered by another player’s action or are wasted. You must therefore choose the characters you need while anticipating what others may choose, and pay attention to the order in which you play them. This also balances turn order: someone who plays a character first has the advantage of choosing a desired building before others, but runs a greater risk of opponents sharing the action and reducing its benefit. Glass Road’s design is very clean—perhaps Uwe devoted most of his energy to Caverna—and its strategic depth is not especially high. This lighter title is genuinely a good introduction to his style for new players.

Caverna

Third! Third! I am not obsessed with rankings, but watching Uwe’s Caverna climb steadily still made me a little excited. Its design is fundamentally based on Agricola. It removes occupations and minor improvements, then makes many buildings available for purchase from the beginning, as in Ora. Several details, including fencing and feeding, are simplified. It introduces expeditions, which effectively give players a more flexible action space; to keep them balanced, the dwarf with the lowest weapon level must act first. Scoring is also more exhilarating. Agricola’s scoring caps disappear, and even unusual development paths can produce high scores, helping distinguish players’ strategies. These details show that Caverna inherited the ideas of Uwe’s earlier designs and truly brought them together. It is therefore unsurprising that it replaced Agricola as his highest-ranked game.

Summary

As this article approaches its end, I want to quote a line I once read that expresses how I feel about Uwe’s game design. “Game design is an art, and every art has its own language. In game design, mechanisms are the main elements of that language. They are like words: ordinary people can use them only to write ordinary prose, but Shakespeare in literature, or Uwe Rosenberg and Stefan Feld in board games, can use them to create masterpieces.” What makes Uwe even more remarkable is his continual refinement. He gradually distills and innovates upon a simple mechanism until it approaches perfection and elegance. Looking back, it may no longer resemble the original idea, yet its roots can always be traced.

Afterword

I had long wanted to write about Uwe’s games but never acted on the idea. First, I truly had not played every one of them deeply. I had only read an introduction to At the Gates of Loyang and never played it at all. Nor am I a prominent figure in the board-game community, merely an ordinary player, so I never felt qualified. Second, Uwe’s games form such a distinctive school that a poorly written article could draw fire from both supporters and detractors. Last month I saw Yikeguan’s call for writers and, after much thought, decided to try. A recent family move had left little time for writing, but looking at the wall of board games in my new study finally gave me motivation to record my modest view of Uwe’s work. I deliberately omitted Ora et Labora’s weaknesses because I simply could not bear to criticize my favorite game. I must thank the friends who loved Ora as much as I did and whom I met during my relatively free junior and senior years at university. I still remember how, at the end of many sessions, we somehow tied. Repeated ties are astonishing in a game where scores reach into the hundreds. I also feel fortunate not to have missed such an excellent game. (As far as I knew, Ora had never been reprinted and was almost impossible to buy. Good news! Game Harbor has licensed a Chinese edition of Ora et Labora, expected early next year. Its quality should be improved in the same way as the second German edition.)
