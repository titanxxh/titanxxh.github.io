Little World, Big World

titanxxh2021-10-302026-08-22

Epigraph: a child’s big world may be only a little world to an adult, but I hope every adult can find joy inside it and be happy together with the child.

Before You Were Born

He and she had just returned from their long honeymoon and were happily planning future journeys to see the wonders of the world, as though the whole globe lay beneath their feet. Not long afterward, she became pregnant. At that moment, he did not understand what awaited him, because he had almost no memories of his own childhood. Perhaps welcoming a little life would not change his own very much, he thought hopefully.

She had no particularly severe reactions during pregnancy, and every prenatal examination was normal. Perhaps you were one of those angel babies displayed on Xiaohongshu. They continued eating, drinking, and going out much as before, even traveling with her large belly and visiting Faxi Temple to make a wish.

Yet the world does not always move as he wishes. He did not know that over the next few days his world would change completely, its boundaries contracting to a tiny hospital room where day and night became indistinguishable.

Everyone had expected the due date to be delayed, but ruptured membranes meant she had to enter the delivery room early. He paced anxiously outside. The number of husbands waiting there fell from more than a dozen to several and finally to him alone. Night had deepened when someone suddenly told him that an emergency cesarean was necessary. Still dazed, he remembered only a few keywords from everything the doctor said, then signed his name with a trembling hand and watched the little window close. He hurried to the operating room and stared at the sole name on the display. He tried opening a wiki to search for information and calm himself, but failed. Tears finally escaped beyond restraint. The operation was actually quick, but to him it felt exceptionally long. Only when a nurse appeared at the window and told him that mother and child were both fine did he exhale. Yes: he had become a father.

The First Five Days

Dad stayed awake to monitor whether Mom’s IV had finished and kept waiting until after six in the morning, when your observation finally ended and you were brought to her. He stared at your tiny face for a long time and genuinely could not find a single feature resembling his own. Only many days later, after returning home and comparing your face with photographs of himself as a newborn, did he understand what an uncanny resemblance meant.

To be honest, you looked a little ugly immediately after birth. But all the nurses said you were impressive. You could cry real tears from the moment you arrived and were exceptionally good at drinking milk. Lying against Mom, you quickly learned the trick without hurting her. Everyone says that newborns lose weight during their first few days, but yours had already stopped falling on the second day. Amazing!

Because of the pandemic, only one family member could accompany Mom and that person could not be replaced. Dad remembers squeezing sleep into every available gap during those days and at one point feeling almost neurasthenic. Yet on the day of discharge, he became excited instead. Watching you wrapped little by little in a tiny blanket, then carrying you through the hospital doors, Dad felt that he might someday become the person you relied upon most after Mom.

The First Three Months

Science still had to be trusted: such a tiny baby did not recognize people at all. During your first two months, your world was confined to the crib and the thirty centimeters within your vision. Mom, Grandma, and the maternity nanny were the people nearest to you. Dad quickly fell out of the rankings.

At three months, your active nature gradually revealed itself. You lifted your head and rolled over ahead of other babies. A crib could no longer satisfy your world, and wherever you were, you seemed eager to try moving. You were still tiny enough for Dad to place you on his thighs and guide your limbs through passive exercises. You responded with a barbell-like laugh and the simplest interaction. Since your hair had been shaved, from certain angles you looked exactly as Dad had in childhood—so much so that he posted the comparison on Instagram.

Looking back over those first hundred days, Dad found everything about you wonderful. Only one problem kept you from becoming one of the angel babies on Xiaohongshu: you were a terrible sleeper. Dad seems to have successfully put you to sleep only once, but you slept so well that time that he excitedly announced the achievement to everyone. Babies at this age cannot avoid feeding at night and generally sleep poorly, so Mom and Dad did not yet consider it a serious problem.

Before You Could Walk

Your advanced gross-motor development soon let you crawl forward, so Mom built you a little realm of your own. Dad, however, only wanted to sing: “Youzi, Youzi, you are extraordinary. A little playpen cannot hold you; crawl out and become an athlete.” From the beginning, you wanted to escape. We still remember your first attempt. Your short arms and legs hung in the air before you landed face-first, completing a thoroughly undignified jailbreak. Grandma simply watched from beside you—and even filmed it.

To make your little world inside the playpen more entertaining, Dad began teaching you how to use toys. You never grasped what adults considered the important part, so Dad started finding his own entertainment. With a ring-stacking toy, for example, he taught sorting algorithms to a baby who did not even understand relative size. You nevertheless appeared to listen very seriously. Who knows whether you will retrieve any of this knowledge from the depths of memory when you grow up?

By then, you could say “Dad” and “Mom” without conscious intention, but what you loved most was imitating random sounds. Whenever Mom and Dad taught you something, you learned their onomatopoeia fastest. At first, Mom did not know where that trait came from. Later, your great-aunts visited, demonstrated their own vocal sound effects, and revealed it as a traditional talent of the Xu family.

As during your first three months, you remained a terrible sleeper. Mom and Dad were both experts at sleep and could not understand why, so Dad entered a period of anxiety—mostly expressed aloud. He spent every day saying, “You still cannot sleep through the night. What are we going to do?” Then during the National Day holiday, you suddenly slept through several nights. Dad thought you had finally awakened to the idea and prepared to celebrate, only to discover that it had been an accident. From then on, he completely accepted the truth. If you were a bad sleeper, so be it. Do whatever you want.

Learning to Walk

Because the crawling mat had a fence, at a little over eight months you could pull yourself upright against it. The adults then gave you objects requiring both hands, causing you to release the fence unconsciously. Gradually, you learned to stand independently. You already moved very quickly while holding the fence and could circle the playpen within seconds. Dad’s long observation showed that your legs were strong enough, while Grandpa exclaimed at the force of your little feet, far surpassing the limp children we encountered outside. Everything suggested that you possessed all the prerequisites for independent walking. Inside the playpen, Dad would move quickly from one corner to the opposite one, using your desire to reach him as walking practice. At first, you took only one or two steps before recognizing the danger and grabbing the nearest fence. Dad moved to another corner, and you tried again. Within two days, you could cross almost the full diagonal. Greed spoiled only the final few steps: instead of walking them, you threw yourself directly into Dad’s arms.

Even before you learned to walk, the tiny fenced world could no longer satisfy your desire to explore. Afterward, you raced throughout the home. Grandpa followed constantly, afraid that you would fall, but you were actually very steady. The little anti-fall backpack we bought was used only a few times because you could walk perfectly well. Looking back, Mom and Dad seem never to have told you to walk slowly while you were learning. Perhaps that is why you became bolder than children your age. “Trying to run before learning to walk” describes you exactly.

Playing “Together”

Children truly see the world differently from adults. Mom filled your little realm with all kinds of classic toys, but at first you never used them as adults expected and instead formed your own interpretations. So although we called it playing together, Dad often played by himself in your little world while you played by yourself beside him. Then, at some particular moment, you might notice that his activity looked interesting and come to interact.

Take the building blocks at home. At first, Dad tried to teach you how to build shapes. The moment he stacked one or two pieces, you knocked them down. He eventually gave up and invented other games for himself. One time, he arranged blocks of different shapes in a single layer on the round lid of their container, trying to fit as many as possible by using space efficiently. You watched him rearrange existing blocks to make room for one more. When Dad began a second layer, you seemed to understand and joined in, performing the same process yourself.

Mom also bought you a “busy block.” Each of its six faces held a familiar object involving rotation, including a key in a lock, a fidget spinner, and a screw and nut. It was intended to keep you busy on planes and high-speed trains. But perhaps you could not understand the correct way to use the objects or why the consequences of those actions mattered. You applied only brute force, found no enjoyment, and defeated the keep-you-busy plan. Later, Dad played with the block himself. He tilted it so that the spinning fidget spinner struck the hanging key. You may have watched the key spring upward, fall, and be launched again and found it interesting. You began striking the spinner with your hand to keep it rotating. Once you understood rotation, you also learned that repeatedly hitting the nut with one hand made it climb the screw, while hitting it with the other made it descend.

Of course, there were even more toys that we could never teach you to use, such as the shape sorter we demonstrated for six months. Looking back now, perhaps Dad had never found the fun in it either.

Afterword

In the blink of an eye, this article was delayed until you were nearly eighteen months old, entirely because Dad was too lazy. Looking back over that journey, Dad has thought more deeply about what raising a new life means to a person. As he accompanied you, watched your world grow from small to large, and played “together” with you, Dad felt as though he too was growing beside you, rediscovering the joy of childhood—genuine joy.

Written as Dad’s Thirtieth Birthday Approaches

While Dad recently organized photographs of his and Mom’s childhoods, an amusing imagined conversation between father and daughter kept appearing in his mind.

After seeing a childhood picture of Dad, his daughter curiously asks for the little boy’s story. Dad might tell it like this:

“There was once a little boy. In primary school, his world was a tiny football field, and even heatstroke could not stop him from sweating beneath the sun. The year 2002 gave him his perfect football memory: China reached the World Cup for the first time, Brazil completed seven victorious matches, and Ronaldo returned as king with eight goals and the Golden Boot.

“In middle school, he moved to a large city. Algorithm problems on a small screen became another interest. Even when failure to solve one meant punishment laps, he happily remained in the computer room solving problems on the online judge. He now made his own decisions and actively explored the larger world beyond his textbooks.

“Later, he entered university, where every kind of board game became his new passion. He left behind the lonely little world of video games played alone. In Reiner Knizia’s auction games, he could experience schemes and intrigue; on Uwe Rosenberg’s farm, he could score points while going hungry. He could deceive rivals and rule from the Iron Throne in A Game of Thrones, or manage railway routes while manipulating company share prices and experiencing the rises and falls of commerce in a train game.

“Then he met the love of his life. His favorite thing became doing everything with her: plays, musicals, art exhibitions, making pottery, and traveling—riding the Star Ferry in Hong Kong, getting soaked together on campus during the World Cup final, and taking an extraordinarily long honeymoon across Iberia.”

“And then what happened?”

What happened next returns us to the beginning of this article.
