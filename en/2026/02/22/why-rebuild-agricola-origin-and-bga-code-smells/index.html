[Open Agricola Development Diary] EP 1: Why Rebuild Agricola?

titanxxh2026-02-222026-08-29

Agricola is more than a decade old. By today’s standards, it is not especially heavy. How hard could a complete digital version really be?

Its rulebook is not frighteningly thick. The trouble is that a lot of traps can fit inside one sentence. Take Hammer Crusher:

Immediately before you renovate to stone, you get 2 clay and 1 reed and you can take a “Build Rooms” action.

I glanced at that text and thought: what is so hard about this? Hand out the resources, let the player build, done. Once a program has to run it, the questions arrive immediately. Exactly when does it trigger? Can someone who cannot yet afford a stone renovation choose Renovation in the first place? How many more traps can you spot? I spotted none the first time. I will come back to them later.

This afternoon, I made the first commit to Open Agricola. Before I started, my thoughts kept returning to the card that had already caused me so much pain.

A Farm I Can Never Finish

I found Agricola soon after getting into board games. Back then it felt genuinely heavy: feed a family, grow crops and animals, and somehow turn a broken wooden hut into a respectable farm before fourteen rounds were over. It was unlike the games I had played before. The game might end at the table, but the replay in my head often ran for several more days.

From face-to-face games at university to nearly 200 slow matches on iOS, my score did rise, just never in a straight line. At first, even a win might score only a little over 40, while a disrupted plan could leave me in the twenties. Later I occasionally reached 50-plus or even broke 60, then got stuck somewhere new. In my 2017 essay, The Art and Way of Agricola, I described the process as three levels of understanding. By the end, I thought action efficiency mattered most. Even now, I do not feel that I have finished this game, much less understood it completely.

BGA Brought an Old Game Back to Life

In the iOS era, reliably gathering four players was difficult, and many matches crawled forward over an entire week. BGA made it much easier. Starting a farm no longer required calling for players in a chat group and waiting. There were more opponents, games started faster, and unfamiliar decks and strange positions appeared all the time.

As the game count grew, the players grew stronger together. Ideas once shared within a small group were quickly tested again by a much larger player pool. A strategy I thought was mature could meet a stronger opponent and be sent straight back to training. My average score still rose, stalled, then rose again. I used to advance mostly by making my own mistakes. Now the constantly evolving understanding of Agricola players around the world pushed me along as well.

Incidentally, the idea that every old game must slowly lose its players does not fit Agricola at all. On the day I begin Open Agricola, it remains one of BGA’s most popular heavy strategy games. More games mean more strange combinations and rules disputes, and even expert players still run into unfamiliar situations. I have kept playing all these years, and there is still more to learn.

From User to Developer

Several years ago, when the E deck was being brought to BGA, a group of Chinese players volunteered to join Agricola’s development team, and I was one of them. From September 2023 to June 2024, I made 105 commits during an intense stretch of development. At first, the work mainly involved bringing a batch of E-deck cards into the game. It quickly turned into fixing combinations, covering edge cases found in real matches, and maintaining messy logic coupled together in several places. Implementing one card feels satisfying. Maintaining dozens of cards that can all affect one another brings a very different kind of pain. This was also my first encounter with the legendary “best language”: PHP. There were no AI agents to help at the time, so I had to grit my teeth, read the code piece by piece, and work through each implementation step by step. Pain level: x2.

Because I was already a programmer, I skipped the low-hanging fruit of simple cards from the beginning and searched the spreadsheet for complex cards that had not yet been implemented. As luck would have it, the second card I chose was Hammer Crusher, the card from the opening. Pain level: x3.

Hammer Crusher Took More Than One Pass

Now back to the card from the opening. Hammer Crusher is the card I remember best. At the time, I also thought it did nothing more than insert one small action before renovation, something I could surely finish in half a day. Then the game slapped me down again.

The real trouble lies in these seven questions:

  1. Does “before renovation” mean before checking whether renovation is possible, or after the player has chosen to renovate but before paying the cost?
  2. If the player cannot initially renovate to stone, but the 2 clay and 1 reed granted by the card make it possible, should the system first allow an action that currently appears impossible?
  3. What if the granted resources are not enough by themselves, but become enough when combined with effects from other cards? How can the system know that in advance?
  4. Once the effect begins, how does the system ensure that the player must renovate to stone rather than switch to clay?
  5. What happens if the player spends the required resources during the extra room build and invalidates the prerequisite for renovating to stone?
  6. If the extra room build triggers another player’s card, what happens when the flow fails to switch to the correct acting player?
  7. After control switches back, what happens if the renovation prerequisite is no longer satisfied?

It was first implemented in March 2023, temporarily disabled over boundary problems that September, and restored after its combination logic was fixed. It was disabled again in January 2024, then returned after another fix in February. The card had one sentence on it, yet I spent almost a year returning to it. The text did not change. It simply encountered more combinations, and the holes I had missed kept showing up.

Some of the blame is mine, and I have no right to dump all of it on the old code. When I built the card, I just wanted players to use it as soon as possible. Adding one special case really was the easiest choice in the moment. The trouble began when the next card did the same thing. Every later change to renovation or room building meant digging through those branches again and hoping I had not missed one.

BGA’s Answers and Its Baggage

BGA has run an enormous number of real games, and that is valuable. Whenever players encounter something the rulebook never fully explained, the program still has to answer: when does a card trigger, how do payment options combine, what happens after a cancellation, and which card resolves first? Its interface answered another practical question for me too: what does the player need to see and choose at each step?

The code is another matter. A football club that has played for years will inevitably have temporary arrangements left by several managers on its tactics board. They may have won matches at the time, but a new club cannot simply copy them. BGA’s code felt much the same to me: many choices have a history and solved real problems, but I would not carry them unchanged into Open Agricola.

Five Code Smells in BGA

While deciding the direction of Open Agricola, I went through BGA’s implementation again. These five things bothered me most:

  1. Changing one card often means editing several files. Its trigger, choice, resolution, and cleanup may all live in different places. After changing one piece of the rule, the developer has to find the rest and make sure they still agree.
  2. Shared code contains too many checks for specific cards. Renovation knows about one card, room building knows about another, and each gets its own branch. Once enough exceptions accumulate, who dares touch the shared flow?
  3. There is plenty of global state, but no consistent way to represent a card’s own state. Complex cards need to preserve choices and progress across several steps. Where that data belongs, what it should be called, and when it should be cleared are not clearly defined.
  4. Card resolution order has to be specified by hand. When discounts, alternative payments, and modifiers collide, the developer writes the partial order between cards manually. Adding a card often means adding its relationships with old cards too. Miss one, reverse one, or create a conflict, and resolution happens in the wrong order.
  5. Triggers after cooking are particularly awkward. The cooking and exchange flow has no stable place for them. With D056, the implementation cannot grant the extra food when cooking is detected. It first records the reward in card state, then waits for a general response window to grant it later. One event becomes “record, wait, grant,” and there was even a bug where the card listened before it had been played.

I eventually started calling this bill the expansion tax. A new card pays for its own implementation and for old exceptions left in shared flows. The larger the card pool becomes, the harder the bill is to calculate. Eventually, adding one card is no longer a one-file job.

Afterword

After that detour, I am back at the opening question: why start Open Agricola? The most immediate reason is that I want to try building a cleaner architecture from scratch, so future cards do not keep paying this expansion tax. The other reason is simple. I want to use a project I know well, and one difficult enough to be interesting, to find out how far Vibe Coding can actually carry development.

There is another practical reason: contributing is not the same as owning. BGA’s Agricola implementation is constrained by the platform and its licenses. The code cannot be public, and we do not decide which expansions may be added or when work begins. That is not BGA’s fault; licensing and scheduling are normal concerns for a commercial platform. From my side, though, I still want a public implementation that anyone can read and verify, with an expansion roadmap I can choose for myself.

BTW, Open Agricola itself still has not received formal authorization. I have sent Uwe Rosenberg a private message, but he has not replied.

Writing this brought me back to the action efficiency I wrote about years ago. In Agricola, a strong hand can still produce a low score. Code works the same way: finishing one card in half a day does not mean the project is moving quickly. Special cases pushed into shared flows to save time will show up again during the next change.

I do not know how far Open Agricola will go. But if I really want to fit more cards and expansions into it, the code cannot keep moving forward on “just get this card working first.” Otherwise, the year I spent wrestling with Hammer Crusher will simply happen again.
