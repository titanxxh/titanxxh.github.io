[Open Agricola Development Diary] EP 4: How the Full Expansion Got Done

titanxxh2026-10-012026-10-06

Open Agricola has now reached the goal it set out with: every known expansion of the revised edition. The first line of the README says “supports every Agricola expansion”, and that sentence is finally no longer a wish.

At the start of the first episode I asked: this game is more than a decade old, so how hard could a complete digital version really be? Back then, all I had was a rough sense of the complexity that Hammer Crusher, the card that cost me a year, would bring in. Now a thousand cards are done, so I can look back at where the difficulty was and how it was solved: how the project went from the previous episode’s “starting to look like a real project” to the full expansion on the table.

Chew the Hard Ones First, Then Batch the Rest

The failed explorations early on taught me that cards do not get built at a steady pace. The beginning is slow, with most of the time going into a handful of hard cards; once the mechanism flywheel is complete, the remaining hundreds are laid out in batches; and after that, all that is left is “getting the already implemented cards right”, with almost no human involvement.

So what this section is really about is which cards forced which architectural complexity. A few of the important ones:

  • New phases. The engine originally left eight phases on an action; three more were forced by cards. The anytime phase exists for the batch of “at any time you may…” cards; Conservator forced a phase for injecting candidates into a choice list; cards like Cookery Extension, which bring their own exchanges, forced a phase for injecting exchanges.
  • State is written only through leaves. First came effects like Child Ombudsman that a player may refuse: the engine had to leave no trace when the player declined, so a dedicated state-writing special-effect leaf appeared. Later Mud Wallower’s counter got incremented twice, and this became a hard rule: a listener never writes state directly. More on that next episode.
  • Fences and stables are resources too. Market Booth, Loppers, Open Air Farmer, Stable Planner, Lazybones, Master Fencer: six cards spend fences like money. The limits of 15 fences and 4 stables used to be hard-coded; aligning these cards broke all of it, so fences and stables joined the payment resources and became an invariant.
  • Extra turns. Adoptive Parents lets a newborn act in the round it is born, which gave the turn rotation an extra-turn extension point, and the project its first architecture decision record.
  • Card as field. Cards like Wood Field and Rock Garden, where the card itself is a field, forced a declarative cardField; later Stable Manure split “field” into three terms, logical field, farmyard field and card field, and within five days there was a boundary, a decision record and a gate.
  • Rollback of nested actions. Hammer Crusher plus Publican, covered in the next section.

To be fair, Agricola’s card design is wilder than most games’. Many games weaken card individuality for the sake of mathematical models and balance, so their cards are not that complex to implement (Ark Nova, for example).

Only once the architecture flywheel was in place did batching begin. AGENTS.md spells out three judgements to make before touching a card: is this a local fix, a reuse of existing hooks, or a missing generic mechanism; if infrastructure is missing, design the extension first, and never push a single card’s if-else into the main path; and if the main path really must change, say which cards it serves.

Hammer Crusher: The Nightmare Returns

The figure above is the one from episode 1. Time to reveal an easter egg: the five nodes outlined in violet in EP 1 were the places where BGA’s implementation changed shared code outside the card file, in three modules: renovation, construction and player card management. All five are now generic engine mechanisms, so the outlines turned green.

Episode 1 listed seven pitfalls for this card. Let me go through them one by one: how each is handled now, and why only this card’s own file had to change. Two terms first. The engine fixes the execution order of every action, and a card may only attach a read-only piece of logic, a listener, at a few phases along that order; only the action leaf inside the action body changes state. This card uses two phases: before, which runs before the action, and isDoable, which decides whether the action can be taken.

  1. When exactly is “before renovating”? The engine decides that for every card: after the player picks a renovation target and before the strict doability check, there is a before phase. The card merely declares that it hooks the before of the renovation action, checks whether the target is stone, and if so returns a flow: gain 2 clay and 1 reed, then an optional room build. About ten lines.
  2. The player cannot afford the stone house yet; admit them anyway? The card hooks a second phase, isDoable: if the target is stone, it answers “doable”. That only gets them through the door. After before has run, the engine checks strictly again against the real state, and if they still cannot pay, they still cannot enter.
  3. The gift is not enough on its own, only together with other cards; how to predict that in advance? Don’t. The engine first lets this card and the other cards at the same phase really execute their before effects, then computes costs and feasibility on the real state; the other cards’ discounts go through the same cost pipeline, the approach from the previous episode that applies every rule repeatedly in every order instead of sorting them. The card needs to know nothing.
  4. How to lock in that this renovation must end in stone? The target is chosen before before runs, and every later phase sees the same target; the card simply reads that locked target, without remembering anything itself, and without worrying that the player takes the reward and switches to clay.
  5. The extra build spends the resources needed for renovating. The build is an optional construct leaf in the flow, which runs the normal pipeline again as an ordinary action. The moment it breaks the renovation’s preconditions, the engine’s strict probe at the next command boundary catches it: the engine enters an invalid state and forces the player to roll back.
  6. It triggers another player’s card, so the acting player must switch. The after phase of construction is generic dispatch; Building Tycoon declares on its own that it listens to opponents. Handing control over, letting the opponent choose and switching back is all the engine’s cross-player execution.
  7. After switching back, the preconditions for renovating no longer hold. This is the most important of the seven, so it gets its own paragraphs.

At the moment control is handed over, the renovation has been committed to but has not actually happened; when the opponent finishes choosing and control comes back, it may no longer be possible. The engine’s answer: at the entry of the command where control first leaves, it takes a checkpoint and opens a provisional scope, and everything that happens from there is provisional. When control returns, it probes strictly once; if the renovation is still doable, the provisional effects become final; if not, the whole scope rolls back to the checkpoint, restoring game state, engine cursors, events, hidden information and random numbers together, and the player is back at the room plan to choose again. A failed plan is remembered, so the same plan never bothers the opponent twice. Scopes nest by level, and a failed child scope rolls back only itself; once the renovation is probed as doable, a guard is placed and later commands may no longer break it.

You might ask whether this much machinery is necessary. Is it over-engineering? If the mechanism had been written for Hammer Crusher alone, with no real abstraction, it would be no different from a single-card special case.

What I can say is that it is not over-engineering: almost every player-switching scenario tied to a before effect has the same problem. Publican is another example: before another player takes an unconditional sow action, Publican’s owner may give them 1 grain for 1 point. Can a player with an empty field and no grain choose to sow first and wait for the gift? In BGA’s implementation they cannot. The ruling on that card is explicit: if the only way the sower could sow is with Publican’s grain, the offer is not made at all, because once the giver declines, a mandatory sow action is stuck with no way back. With rollback, Open Agricola lets the player provisionally pick sow; if the offer is declined, they return to the action choice.

Two cards, one pitfall, one mechanism.

Of the seven pitfalls, the card file is responsible only for the declarations behind the first two: which phases to hook, and whether the target is stone. The file is 61 lines with two listeners. The other five are built into the engine’s generic mechanisms.

Of course, this time was not a single pass either. The rollback was only truly settled near the end of summer, and was revised once after that. It comes with a session test of 15 cases. The difference is where the fix lives: this time it went into a generic engine mechanism, and the next card can use it directly.

Afterword

The question from the start of episode 1 can be answered now. The hard part of a complete digital Agricola is not building one card; it is reaching the thousandth card with the project’s architecture still working efficiently.
